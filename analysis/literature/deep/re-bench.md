## Section-by-section notes

**Abstract.** 7 challenging, open-ended ML research engineering environments; 71 8-hour attempts by 61 distinct human experts. 82% of expert attempts achieve non-zero score; 24% match/exceed the strong reference solutions. Best AI agents score 4x humans at a 2-hour total budget; humans narrowly exceed agents at 8 hours and reach 2x the top agent at 32 total hours (best-of-k across attempts). An agent wrote a faster custom Triton kernel than any of the 9 human experts. Environments, human data, analysis code, and agent trajectories open-sourced (github.com/METR/ai-rd-tasks; transcripts.metr.org).

**1 Introduction.** Motivation: AI R&D automation is a frontier-safety-policy trigger capability. Logic of the design: "if AI agents perform significantly worse than human ML experts given equivalent resources and conditions, then the AI agents likely cannot automate these experts' research work" — one-directional inference (agent > human on the suite does NOT imply automation capability). Contributions: 7 novel hand-crafted environments; 71 attempts by 61 experts under agent-equivalent conditions; score@k results for o1-preview and Claude 3.5 Sonnet in two scaffolds; qualitative analysis. Most agent solutions score ~0, but agents submit solutions >10x faster than humans; both o1-preview and Claude 3.5 Sonnet found kernel solutions beating all 9 human experts.

**2 Background. 2.1 Risks:** runaway feedback loops of AI doing AI R&D; weight theft; self-improvement into other risk domains; sabotage of oversight if misaligned. Full automation is both where acute risk concentrates and conceptually simpler to evaluate ("demonstrating any critical missing capability is sufficient"). **2.2 Early warning evaluations** need three properties: feasibility (they cite SWE-bench items turning out impossible/under-specified even in the verified subset), ecological validity (unclear how MLE-bench scores map to automation risk), and resistance to saturation (most benchmarks saturate fast). **2.3 Related work + Table 1:** compares 10 benchmarks on novelty/contamination, human comparisons, task count, time horizon: MLE-bench (75 tasks, contaminated, ~human comp), Sci-code (338), GPQA (448, 30m), GAIA (466, 5–20m), SWE-bench verified (500, 5m–4h, contaminated), WebArena (812, 2m), ML-Agent-Bench (13), DISCOVERYWORLD (120, 1h), H-ARC-AGI (800, 5m), RE-Bench (7 tasks, 8h). RE-Bench is the only one that is both novel/non-contaminated and directly human-compared at an 8-hour horizon.

**3 RE-Bench. 3.1 Design goals:** two practical constraints (8-hour expert runs; <=8 H100s) and two pillars: (i) maximize coverage of AI R&D challenge types, (ii) experts reliably make steady progress toward a high ceiling. **3.2.1 Environments (Table 2):** 7 environments in 3 families — Optimize runtime (Optimize LLM Foundry; Optimize a Kernel), Optimize loss (Fix Embedding; Scaling Law Experiment; Restricted Architecture MLM), Optimize win-rate (Finetune GPT-2 for QA, judged by Llama-3-8B; Scaffolding for Rust CodeContests, 175 held-out problems). Each environment ships a runnable scoring function with a timestamped score log, a valid-but-weak starting solution, and a hidden author-written reference solution. Final score = max over score log, except Scaling Law (final submission only, score never shown). **3.2.2 Normalization:** y_n = (y - y_s)/(y_r - y_s); starting = 0, reference = 1; sub-starting scores floored at 0; no normalized score above 2 ever observed; human 8h per-environment averages fall in 0.5–1.5. **3.2.3 Creation process:** three review gates (specification, implementation, baselining); they dropped >12 specifications and >5 completed implementations. **3.3 Coverage (Tables 3–4):** environments span 218–1,392 LOC, feedback loops from 40 s (Kernel) to 2.5 h (Fix Embedding); mapped onto Epoch AI's 8 AI-R&D skills; admitted gaps: distributed training orchestration, research direction setting, dataset creation, hardware debugging; researcher interviews add: technical design, novel math/theory, metric creation, generalization demands, ill-defined goals, greater engineering complexity. **3.4 Human experts (Table 5):** 71 runs / 61 experts from 3 sources — ML RS/RE hiring process (43 baseliners, 45 runs, avg 0.48), professional network (11, 17, 0.98), grad student outreach (7, 9, 0.83); overall avg 0.64. (Body text gives 0.96 vs 0.46 for network vs hiring — minor internal inconsistency with Table 5.) They caution against comparing to the average human and prefer best-attempt comparisons. **3.5 Validation (Table 6):** in every environment >70% of experts score >0.05 by 8h; >80% report the score matched their expectation; major technical issues occur in >5% of runs; starting-score SDs mostly 0–0.02 but 0.62 for Finetune GPT-2 for QA (noisy RL + judge); two environments (Scaling Law, Finetune GPT-2) judged saturated-by-experts (top human within ~15% of estimated ceiling).

**4 Experiments.** Vivaria platform; VMs with 20–48 vCPUs, 200–400 GB RAM, 0–6 H100s. Models: claude-3-5-sonnet-20241022, claude-3-5-sonnet-20240620, o1-preview. Scaffolds: METR "Modular" (general-purpose) and AIDE (best in MLE-bench studies); o1-preview run mainly in AIDE after poor Modular pilots. Wall-clock limits (not token limits) so GPU-hours match humans; API-stall time not counted. **4.1 By time:** score-over-time curves from log-linear interpolation over 3–5 8-hour runs/environment; agents progress fast early then plateau; humans start slow, overtake later; AIDE keeps making occasional progress longer than Modular. A Claude dip at 7min–1h traces to one anomalous lucky Scaling Law guess. **4.2 By samples:** best-of-k with replacement (Scaling Law selects randomly since score is hidden). Modular does best as 30-minute runs; AIDE as 2-hour runs. Best agents (o1-preview AIDE; Claude 3.5 Sonnet new, Modular) reach the **36th and 37th percentile of human experts** at an 8h budget. With k=128 30-minute samples, agents still far below top human; same with extra 2-hour samples. Figure 2: at 16h the best allocations were 30min@32 (Claude), 2h@8 (o1-preview), 8h@2 (humans); 95% CIs by percentile bootstrap, "skewed downwards due to a long right tail of agent performance and best-of-k sampling." **4.3 By environment (Figure 9):** at 32h budget, Claude approaches human level on Finetune GPT-2 for QA and Scaling Law (where o1-preview does poorly); o1-preview exceeds humans on Optimize a Kernel.

**5 Qualitative analysis. 5.1 Successes:** both frontier models found kernel solutions ~2x faster than the reference; o1-preview's beat all 9 humans — partly an expertise gap (most humans lacked Triton/kernel background). Agents check score 36.8/hr (AIDE) and 25.3/hr (Modular) vs 3.4/hr for humans; they find tweaked-starting-solution "local optima" that score surprisingly well. Best Finetune GPT-2 score (0.88) fell to 0.69 on rerun — overfitting to evaluation noise. **5.2 Failures:** low solution diversity (84% of analyzed Restricted-MLM attempts used lightly modified transformers despite no-division/no-exp making them terrible; n=37 high-scoring runs manually analyzed); persistent instruction misreadings (some becoming rule-breaking "loopholes" caught only by manual inspection); weak long-horizon agency — stubborn wrong assumptions, failure to clear zombie GPU processes; Modular does better when context is wiped every 30 minutes, i.e., agents accumulate more baggage than insight. **5.3 Small-gap predictors:** short high-fidelity feedback loops, low engineering complexity, specialized-knowledge advantages, noisy scoring, little exploration needed. Empirically LOC-vs-gap R^2 = 0.602; no clear association for feedback-loop length or novelty.

**6 Discussion. 6.1 Why RE-Bench overestimates automation capability:** real AI R&D is >=2 OOM bigger on every dimension (Table 7: time horizon 8h/32h vs 6mo+; feedback loops 2h vs 6mo+; 1,651 vs 1M+ LOC; 1 vs 100+ parallel interacting projects); tasks are cleanly defined and non-interacting; anecdotally agents look further from automation than the modest gap suggests. **6.2 Why it may underestimate:** agents cost ~$123 per 8h run (~29M input + ~499K output tokens) vs ~$1,855 average paid per human expert; agents could win economically while losing per-attempt; alternative AI-native workflows/tooling could bypass human-shaped tasks. **6.3 Other limitations:** design criteria select unrepresentative environments (clear goals, fast feedback, everything provided); estimates noisy given only 7 environments with right-skewed agent scores; high cost/complexity of running (H100s); no scaffold iteration; frontier research increasingly siloed; test-score visibility enables overfitting (they plan validation-only scores in future); Scaling Law rewards lucky guessing. **6.4 Conclusion:** feasible-but-unsaturated suite; agents at ~average-human at 8h best-of-k is "very impressive"; the top-human gap and its closure rate is the early-warning signal.

**References [1]–[59].** Notable: SWE-bench verified (feasibility failures), MLE-bench, GPQA, AIDE technical report, Vivaria, Chinchilla, DPO, LLM Foundry.

## Benchmark anatomy

- **N items:** 7 hand-crafted, open-ended ML research-engineering environments (V1), each an 8-hour, GPU-backed optimization task with a programmatic scoring function. Not a Q&A set: unit of evaluation is a full agent run; results aggregate 3–5+ runs per environment per agent config, and 71 human runs total.
- **Construction:** authored by METR staff/task authors; ideas from ML practitioners, literature, and past work experience; three-gate pipeline (specification review -> implementation + internal solution review -> human baselining with post-hoc adjustment); attrition: >12 specifications and >5 completed implementations dropped.
- **Validation/review:** four explicit validation metrics per environment (fraction of experts >0.05 at 8h — all >70%; expert survey "score matches expectation" — all >80% of respondents; starting-score SD from >300 scoring runs; major-technical-issue count) plus an informal saturation assessment vs speculative expert-week ceilings (Table 8). Issue triage into intended/minor/major with intervention + time compensation for major.
- **Human baseline:** 71 8-hour runs by 61 experts (45 runs from METR job applicants, 17 from professional networks incl. DeepMind/Google/Anthropic/OpenAI/FAR/Redwood alumni with 5+ years relevant experience, 9 from PhD students at Berkeley/CMU/Stanford/MIT); avg pay ~$1,855/run; identical VMs and rules as agents; internet and LLM use allowed; matched to environments by expertise where possible; source-stratified averages 0.98/0.83/0.48 (overall 0.64).
- **Contamination defenses:** novelty by construction — no environment or solution available online (Table 1 "Novel, non-contaminated ✓"); tasks demand experimentation rather than recall; per-environment novelty judgments in B.1. No refresh/held-out plan described; suite is now open-sourced, so the defense decays for future models.
- **Scoring:** environment-specific continuous scoring functions run on demand into a timestamped score log; final = best log entry (except Scaling Law: final submission, score hidden); linear normalization to starting=0/reference=1 with floor at 0; cross-environment mean; score@k = best of k runs sampled with replacement; cheating runs manually zeroed.
- **Judge design:** only Finetune GPT-2 for QA uses an LLM judge (llama-3-8b-instruct pairwise winrate vs gpt2-xl and gpt2-alpaca on held-out prompts). No judge-agreement or judge-robustness analysis is reported; this environment has by far the highest starting-score SD (0.62) and the documented rerun regression (0.88 -> 0.69).
- **Statistical reporting:** 95% CIs via percentile bootstrapping (Figure 2 et al.), explicitly noted as skewed downward by the long right tail + best-of-k; score-over-time via log-linear interpolation averaged over 3–5 runs; no hypothesis tests, no clustered errors (71 runs from 61 experts; environment-level correlation unmodeled), one R^2 (0.602) for LOC-vs-gap across 7 points.

## Reported results

- 82% of 71 expert attempts achieve non-zero score; 24% match/exceed reference solutions (abstract).
- Agents 4x human score at 2h total budget; humans narrowly exceed agents at 8h; humans 2x top agent at 32h (Fig 2; 95% percentile-bootstrap CIs shown as shaded bands, no numeric CI values in text).
- Best agents = 36th (o1-preview, AIDE) and 37th (Claude 3.5 Sonnet 20241022, Modular) percentile of human experts at 8h best-of-k (Sec 4.2).
- Best time allocation at 16h: Claude 30min@32, o1-preview 2h@8, humans 8h@2 (Fig 2 caption).
- k=128 30-minute samples: both Claude versions still improving but "far from top human performance" (Fig 7); same qualitative result for 2-hour samples (Fig 8).
- Scoring frequency: 36.8/hr (AIDE), 25.3/hr (Modular), 3.4/hr (humans) (Sec 5.1).
- Cost: ~$123 per agent 8h run (~29M input + ~499K output tokens) vs ~$1,855 average human payment (Sec 6.2, Fig 11 — at equal dollar budgets agents get >10x the compute-hours).
- Output speed: 20–120 tokens/s across most runs (Fig 12); majority of wall-clock spent waiting on VM commands.
- Kernel: o1-preview 0.64 ms vs 4.74 ms start (Fig 18), ~2x faster than reference, beats all 9 human experts (Secs 1, 5.1).
- Per-environment best-achieved normalized scores (Table 8): Scaling Law 1.17, Kernel 1.81, Fix Embedding 1.05, Rust 1.33, Restricted MLM 1.78, LLM Foundry 0.97, GPT-2 QA 1.43 — vs speculative expert-week ceilings 1.27–1.36, 2.07–2.53, 1.14–1.25, 1.34–1.81, 2.16–2.51, 1.15–1.80, 1.45–1.48.
- Validation (Table 6): experts >0.05 at 8h — 7/8, 7/9, 10/13, 8/11, 10/11, 8/8, 10/11 per environment; expectation-match 6/8 to 9/9; major issues 0–2 per environment (>5% of runs overall); starting-score SD 0.004, 0.005, 0.0, 0, 0.02, 0.08, 0.62.
- Human source gap: professional network 0.98 (n=17 runs) vs hiring applicants 0.48 (n=45) vs grad students 0.83 (n=9); overall 0.64 (Table 5).
- Engineering complexity (LOC of reference-edited files) vs human-agent gap: R^2 = 0.602 across 7 environments (Fig 10).
- Restricted MLM solution homogeneity: 84% transformer variants among 37 manually analyzed high-scoring runs (Sec 5.2 fn 9).
- GPT-2 QA noise: best agent 0.88 normalized -> 0.69 on rerun; winrate 88% -> 69% (Sec 5.1, Fig 21).
- Reference-vs-start anchors per environment: 272 s -> 94 s (Foundry), 5 ms -> Triton reference with <1 ms possible (Kernel), 10.5 -> <2.7 loss (Fix Embedding), 0.24 starting score (Scaling Law), 7.64 -> conv-net reference (MLM), ~0.5 winrate start (GPT-2 QA), 0% -> 12.7% (Rust).

## Limitations

**Admitted by authors (Secs 6.1–6.3, App A/B):**
- Environments are >=2 OOM smaller than real AI R&D on time horizon, feedback-loop length, LOC, and parallel projects (Table 7); tasks cleanly defined and non-interacting; performance here likely overestimates automation capability.
- Design criteria (clear instructions, fast feedback, everything provided, easy to build/assess) make environments unrepresentative of real research.
- Only 7 environments; agent score distributions heavily right-skewed (most runs 0, rare large successes) so estimates are noise-sensitive.
- Expensive and complex to run (hours of H100 time), limiting scale and accessibility.
- No scaffold iteration; better tooling/token-hungry parallel approaches could raise agent scores.
- Frontier research siloing means coverage may diverge from what actually drives frontier progress.
- Test-score visibility permits overfitting (especially GPT-2 QA); they plan validation-only scoring in future versions.
- Scaling Law rewards lucky guessing; agents mostly guess rather than experiment.
- Agents far cheaper than humans, so cost-matched comparisons flip the picture; AI-native workflows might bypass human-shaped tasks entirely.
- Operational caveats: >5% of runs hit major technical issues; break times self-reported by humans; not all runs inspected for cheating.

**Not admitted (my observations):**
- The human baseline is dominated (45/71 runs) by job applicants averaging 0.48 — barely above the weakest cohort's floor — so "36th–37th percentile of human experts" and the 8h crossover are anchored to a mixed-skill pool; against the professional-network cohort (0.98 avg) agents would look much weaker. The paper cautions about this but still headline-reports pooled percentiles.
- No significance testing anywhere; no clustering of uncertainty by expert (repeat baseliners) or by environment; the headline 4x/2x multipliers carry no numeric CIs in text, only shaded plot bands.
- Human best-of-k at 32h = best of four different people's 8h runs; agent best-of-k = resampling the same model. These are different random variables (human k draws new skill from the pool; agent k redraws noise), making the "humans 2x at 32h" comparison conceptually apples-to-oranges.
- The only LLM-judged environment (GPT-2 QA) has no judge-validation (no human agreement rate, no adversarial-gaming audit), yet is one of the two environments where Claude "gets close to human performance" — and is also the noisiest (SD 0.62; 0.88->0.69 rerun regression).
- Cheating screening is asymmetric: only the top 2 runs per environment (top 15 on the two known-gameable ones) were manually inspected, but score@k statistics consume the whole run distribution; undetected mid-tail rule-breaking would inflate agent curves.
- Internal inconsistencies: 0.96/0.46 (text) vs 0.98/0.48 (Table 5); starting kernel time 5 ms (C.2.4) vs 4.74 ms (Fig 18); L1 gate 5.3 (C.1.3) vs 4.3 (Fig 14 transcript); Fig 18 caption "beats some human experts" vs body "beating all 9".
- Open-sourcing the environments spends the novelty/contamination defense for every later model generation; no versioning/refresh protocol is described in the paper.
- Normalization anchors on a single author-written reference solution per environment; cross-environment averages therefore inherit each author's ambition level (reference solutions range from ~saturating to far-from-ceiling per Table 8), and scores >1 let one outlier environment dominate a 7-environment mean.
- Humans were allowed to use LLMs during baselines — the "human" baseline is really human+AI-tools, which biases the human-agent gap in an unquantified direction.

## Implications for CRUCIBLE-CHAIN

1. **Prove feasibility with an explicit validation gate per template, the way RE-Bench did per environment.** RE-Bench's central methodological export is that difficulty claims are worthless without solvability evidence: they required >70% of experts making progress and >80% expectation-match, and cite SWE-bench's impossible items as the canonical failure. After CRUCIBLE-CHAIN strips recipe/menu leaks to reach single-digit pass rates, every hardened template needs a machine-checkable solvability certificate: re-derive the generator's answer using only the exact artifact shown to the model (no generator internals), plus a small human/strong-model-with-tools baseline on a sample. Otherwise "frontier models score 6%" is indistinguishable from "6% of instances are answerable." Adopt their tri-gate pipeline too (spec review -> implementation review -> baselining) and expect RE-Bench-scale attrition (>12 specs and >5 finished implementations dropped for 7 shipped).

2. **Answer the scale question by counting templates, not instances — and bootstrap at the cluster level.** RE-Bench's confessed weakness is n=7 environments with right-skewed scores making "estimates sensitive to sampling noise"; their percentile-bootstrap CIs over so few clusters are wide and skewed. CRUCIBLE-CHAIN's ~18 instances per template share a method recipe and failure mode, so the effective n for between-model claims is ~8, worse than RE-Bench. Concretely: report percentile-bootstrap CIs resampling templates (clusters), not items; for a binomial-ish pass rate in the 3–10% band, distinguishing two models at 95% needs on the order of several hundred effectively-independent items — i.e., 30–60+ independent templates at current instance counts — and adding instances to existing templates buys almost nothing once within-template correlation is high. Measure that intra-template correlation (ICC) in the pilot and publish the design-effect-adjusted n; that is the defensible answer to the question of record.

3. **Set the guessing floor per chain far below the target pass rate, and prove it.** RE-Bench's Scaling Law environment was quietly broken because "random guessing" could land a 0.99: their fix prescription — "A better version of this environment would ensure that random guessing did much worse than real solutions" — is directly a CRUCIBLE-CHAIN spec. For a 5–8 stage non-compensatory chain, compute the analytic guess-through probability (product over stages of picking the right branch by chance given the visible option space) for every template and require it to be, say, <0.5% — otherwise a menu-free stage with a binary judgment call (0.5 prior) times seven stages still passes ~0.8% of the time by luck, which pollutes single-digit measurements. Also run a no-content ablation agent (sees scaffolding, not data) as an empirical floor estimate, RE-Bench-style grounding in the cheap direction.

4. **Never expose the scored signal; expect and instrument best-of-k gaming.** RE-Bench showed models exercise the scorer 25–37x/hour vs 3.4 for humans, ride evaluation noise to inflated bests (0.88 -> 0.69 on rerun), and that visible test scores caused overfitting they now plan to hide ("only provide agents with a validation score... keeping the test score hidden"). For CRUCIBLE-CHAIN: no per-stage correctness feedback during a run, deterministic scoring only after submission; fix the sampling policy (pass@1 at declared temperature) as the headline and report pass@k separately; and rerun/regenerate top-scoring instances (fresh instance from the same template) to detect noise-riding — the generator makes this free, an advantage RE-Bench didn't have.

5. **Automate right-for-wrong-reasons and exploit detection instead of RE-Bench's manual triage.** RE-Bench caught o1-preview copying the reference model only by manually reading top transcripts, and only audited the top 2–15 runs per environment. CRUCIBLE-CHAIN's constructed truth means every stage's correct intermediate value is known: require models to emit stage-level intermediates and score them mechanically; flag any run with a correct final answer but wrong/missing intermediates (lucky or leaked); for planted-defect instances, have the generator emit tell-tale checks whose values differ under the shortcut path vs the legitimate path. This converts RE-Bench's weakest operational step (human transcript review) into a deterministic pipeline.

6. **Measure lure uptake as a first-class metric to prove difficulty is judgment, not obscurity.** RE-Bench found agents overwhelmingly take the attractive default (84% of Restricted-MLM attempts were doomed transformer tweaks) and prefer polishing the starting solution into local optima over rethinking. That is exactly CRUCIBLE-CHAIN's attractive-wrong-path hypothesis — so report, per stage and per model, the fraction of failures that took the designed lure vs failed idiosyncratically. High lure-uptake validates the design ("models fail by taking the seductive path"); low uptake with high failure signals ambiguity or noise, i.e., a SWE-bench-style feasibility defect masquerading as difficulty.

7. **Handle the flawed-premise condition with RE-Bench's own footnote-3 discipline.** RE-Bench demands unambiguous, feasible tasks but concedes: "Some evaluations might deliberately want to test the ability to interpret ambiguous instructions. In this case significant care may be needed to ensure and demonstrate that the correct interpretation can be determined with sufficient skill and effort." For refusal-correct instances, ship a detectability certificate: the generator names the specific inconsistency and the minimal subset of provided observations that reveals it, and a scripted checker verifies the flaw is derivable from the model-visible artifact alone. Calibrate against clean controls to price false refusals (a model refusing everything must score ~0 because it fails all clean/planted items) — the three-condition design already supports this; make the penalty explicit in the scoring spec.

8. **Anchor "hard" claims to stratified baselines, not pooled averages, and pre-register the comparison population.** RE-Bench's pooled human percentile claims blur a 2x gap between hiring applicants (0.48) and professional-network experts (0.98), and the paper itself warns against average-human comparisons. When CRUCIBLE-CHAIN publishes "single-digit frontier pass rates," pair it with (a) a small expert baseline stratified by domain familiarity on sampled instances, and (b) per-condition, per-template breakdowns rather than one pooled number — otherwise the same critique applies: a pooled 6% could hide one saturated template (RE-Bench's Scaling Law problem) plus seven at zero, which has very different scientific meaning and very different variance.
