## Section-by-section notes

**Abstract.** Test-set contamination renders benchmarks obsolete; crowdsourced/LLM-judged benchmarks introduce biases and "break down when scoring hard questions." LiveBench is claimed as the first benchmark that (1) contains frequently-updated questions from recent information sources, (2) scores automatically against objective ground truth, and (3) spans six diverse categories (math, coding, reasoning, language, instruction following, data analysis). Sources: recent math competitions, arXiv papers, news articles, datasets; plus harder contamination-limited versions of Big-Bench Hard, AMPS, IFEval tasks. Evaluates closed models plus dozens of open models 0.5B-405B. Top models score below 70%. Questions added/updated monthly.

**1 Introduction.** Contamination evidence cited: LLM Codeforces performance plummets after training cutoff and pre-cutoff performance correlates with GitHub problem frequency (Roberts et al. 2024; Jain et al. 2024); GSM8K hand-crafted variant reveals overfitting (Zhang et al. 2024). LLM judges are error-prone, favor their own and more verbose answers; human judging injects formatting/tone biases and humans write poorly-constructed, non-diverse prompts. LiveBench: 18 tasks across 6 categories, two task types — (1) information-source-based (Kaggle data analysis, arXiv typo fixing, Guardian articles, recent competitions/movies) and (2) harder/more-diverse versions of existing benchmark tasks (BBH Web of Lies, AMPS, IFEval, LiveCodeBench). Code at github.com/livebench/livebench; leaderboard at livebench.ai.

**2 LiveBench description (intro).** Six categories, "two to four tasks per problem" [sic; per category]. "Each task is designed to have 40-100 questions which span a range of difficulty, from easy to very challenging, while loosely aiming for an overall 30-70% success rate on the top models for each task." Prompts: zero-shot chain of thought, "make its best guess if it does not know," answer output in easy-to-parse form (XML tags or **double asterisks**); the authors acknowledge parsing itself demands instruction following (addressed in Appendix A.4).

**2.1 Math.** Three tasks. Competitions: AMC12 2023 (A and B), SMC 2023, AIME 2024, "modifying the prose and the answer order." Olympiad: USAMO 2024 and IMO 2024, task is to rearrange masked-out equations from the official solution into correct order. AMPS_Hard: synthetic questions from random primitives drawn from "a larger and more challenging distribution than AMPS across 10 of the hardest tasks within AMPS."

**2.2 Coding.** LCB Generation: 78 questions taken from LiveCodeBench (competition problems). Completion: LeetCode easy/medium/hard problems from LCB's April 2024 release paired with kamyu104/LeetCode-Solutions GitHub solutions, "omitting the last 15-70% of each solution" and asking the model to complete it.

**2.3 Reasoning.** Web of Lies v2 (harder BBH variant: extra deductive components, several red-herring types); Spatial (50 handwritten 2D/3D-geometry deduction questions); Zebra Puzzles (procedurally generated from quint-t repository). Includes a full 3-person/3-attribute Zebra example with ground truth "filmmaking."

**2.4 Data analysis.** Three tasks on recent Kaggle/Socrata datasets: CTA (pick the true column name of a sampled column from the table's column-name options), TableReformat (convert among JSON, JSONL, Markdown, CSV, TSV, HTML), TableJoin (produce a valid join mapping between two tables with partially overlapping columns).

**2.5 Instruction following.** Inspired by IFEval; uses 16 of IFEval's 25 verifiable instructions (excluding non-real-world ones; Table 3). Unlike IFEval's bare prompts, models receive a Guardian article and must Paraphrase / Simplify / Summarize / generate a Story subject to multiple randomly drawn instructions. "We score tasks purely by their adherence to the instructions."

**2.6 Language comprehension.** Connections (NYT-style word grouping; 8-, 12-, 16-word varieties); Typos (fix programmatically injected common misspellings in recent arXiv abstracts without changing any other stylistic choice; a full example is given); Plot Unscrambling (reorder randomly shuffled sentences of synopses of movies released after Jan 1, 2024, from IMDb/Wikipedia); the authors note Plot Unscrambling "is very challenging for LLMs."

**2.7 Updates and maintenance plan.** Two updates completed at time of writing. Each update replaces 1/6 of questions on average, so full refresh roughly every 6 months; each month's new questions are withheld from public release for one month so the leaderboard "always has 1/6 questions that are private." Tasks chosen for update: (1) oldest, (2) currently easiest. Sustainability: maintain only the best/most popular 40-50 models (about two versions per model family), i.e., at most 50 models on ~200 new questions monthly — "easily within the computational budgets of the authors' institutions." Completed updates: update 1 added 50 spatial reasoning + 28 coding generation + 12 coding completion questions, bringing the benchmark to 1000 questions; update 2 fully replaced olympiad and partially replaced AMPS_Hard and math_comp — 132 replaced questions, maintaining 1000.

**3 Experiments (setup).** 40 LLMs: proprietary (OpenAI o1-preview/o1-mini/gpt-4o family, Anthropic claude-3/3.5 family, Google gemini-1.5 family and gemini-exp, Mistral, StepFun step-2) and open-source 0.5B-405B (Llama-3.1, deepseek-v2.5, qwen2.5, command-r-plus, gemma-2, mixtral, phi-3.5). Single-turn evaluation, temperature 0 (unless model card says otherwise), FastChat chat templates, bfloat16 for open models. Per-question score in [0,1]; task score = mean over questions; category score = mean over its tasks; LiveBench score = mean over the six categories. Appendix B documents tokens and API cost.

**3.1 Discussion of results.** o1-preview-2024-09-12 best overall at 64.7, "6% better than all other models," and top in data analysis, language, and math. claude-3-5-sonnet-20240620 second (58.2; the 20241022 version 58.5), far best in coding (67.1 for 20241022); o1-mini third (56.7), "significantly better than all other models in the reasoning category" (72.3). Best open models: llama-3.1-405b-instruct (51.1) and qwen2.5-72b-instruct, which "virtually tie" and beat gpt-4-turbo. phi-3.5-moe-instruct (6.6B active parameters) outperforms gpt-3.5 and matches mixtral-8x22b.

**3.2 Correlation analyses.** Pearson correlations across all 40 models: math/coding/reasoning mutually correlated; language correlates with data analysis (both require outputting a modified version of a large prompt portion); instruction following correlates weakly with everything else (0.8174 with the overall average). The text says math_comp correlates highest with overall performance (Table 6 value 0.9035; note Table 6 actually lists web_of_lies_v2 higher at 0.9136 — see my observed limitations). Scatterplots with best-fit lines and residuals: o1 and phi models are reasoning outliers (r=0.944, std_err 0.071 for reasoning vs average); Llama/gemini/command-r are instruction-following outliers (r=0.817, std_err 0.081).

**3.3 Comparison to other benchmarks.** Model-score correlations: LiveBench vs ChatBot Arena 0.91; vs Arena-Hard 0.88. gpt-4-0125-preview and gpt-4-turbo-2024-04-09 do substantially better on Arena-Hard, "likely due to the known bias from using gpt-4 itself as the LLM judge." gemini-1.5's Arena strength attributed to human-preferred output style. Preliminary LLM-judging study teased (details in A.2): if an LLM can't solve a hard math/reasoning question, can it judge an answer to it? "Our experiments give evidence that the answer is yes... but the results are not definitive."

**3.4 Analysis of monthly updates.** Rank correlation between original and first update, and between first and second update, both > 0.997 (rankings stable). Median and mean scores among persistent models dropped ~1.2% from original to most recent question set (benchmark getting harder).

**4 Related work.** HF Open LLM Leaderboard: static, contamination-prone. LLM-as-judge (AlpacaEval, MT-Bench, Arena-Hard): fast and flexible but self-preference bias, judge-family variance, and outright errors (Arena-Hard question 2: GPT-4 judged a correct gpt-4-0314 C++ solution incorrect). Humans-as-judge (ChatBot Arena): labor-intensive, high error rates from annotation design (Lease 2011), high human-to-human variability (Rashkin et al. 2023). LiveCodeBench: rolling + ground truth but coding-only. Omni-MATH: broad but LLM-judged. SEAL (Scale AI): private questions with expert scorers but only 4 categories. Srivastava et al. 2024 functional MATH variants: avoid literal contamination but remain distributionally similar to pretraining data and fixed in hardness. IFEval and Big-Bench(-Hard) described as bases for LiveBench tasks.

**5 Conclusions, limitations, and future work.** Restates the three firsts. Admitted limitations: no non-English tasks yet; ground-truth scoring cannot handle open-ended use cases ("write a travel guide to Hawaii"); residual prompt-format biases favoring certain LLM families; prompts and leaderboard to be updated over time.

**6 Reproducibility statement.** Everything open-sourced (leaderboard, questions, run code, model outputs for 40 models, scoring code) except the newest month's questions, which are withheld one month.

**7 Ethics statement.** No inherently negative societal impacts foreseen; hope the frequently-updated-question framework "will catch on."

**References.** ~100 entries, pp. 11-16 (contamination literature: Roberts et al. 2024, Oren et al. 2023, Golchin & Surdeanu 2023a/b, Singh et al. 2024, Kalal et al. 2024, Zhang et al. 2024; judging literature: Zheng et al. 2024, Li et al. 2024, Dubois et al. 2024; tooling: Sympy, difflib, Levenshtein, Ratcliff/Obershelp).

**A.3.1 Math (details).** Sources and release dates: AMC12A 2023 (Nov 8, 2023), AMC12B 2023 (Nov 14, 2023), SMC 2023 (Oct 3, 2023), AIME I 2024 (Jan 31, 2024), AIME II 2024 (Feb 7, 2024), USAMO 2024 (Mar 20, 2024), IMO 2024 (Jul 22, 2024). **A.3.3 Reasoning (details).** Web of Lies v2: model must evaluate a composed Boolean function expressed in language; hardening = (1) red herrings (names outside the true chain; count drawn uniformly from 0 to 19), (2) asking for three people's truth values instead of one, (3) a location-indirection layer ("Devika is at the museum. The person at the museum says..."); full worked example given (ground truth "no, yes, yes"). Zebra: procedural generator (quint-t 2023) with 10 attribute categories of 15-40 values, constraint "levels" 1-20; LiveBench draws 3 or 4 people (50/50), 3 or 4 attributes (50/50), levels uniform on [10,20]; a solver guarantees a unique solution; author modifications reduce statement ambiguity (e.g., "immediate left"); larger puzzles were "exceedingly difficult" for top LLMs in pretesting. Spatial: 50 handwritten questions; two examples with ground truths "triangle" and "10."

**A.3.4 Data analysis (details).** Table sizes upper-bounded (cell length, column count, row count) due to context limits and cost. CTA: formal relation definition; sample a random table and column, use the actual column name as ground truth, provide 5 sampled cell values with replacement plus all column names as options; Accuracy@1, case-insensitive exact string match. TableReformat: Pandas performs conversions; several JSON variants accepted; response ingested directly into Pandas; accepted only if it passes all tests (column count, row count, exact match on every row's contents); Accuracy@1. TableJoin: build tables A and B from sampled columns with anonymized names in B; F1 over columns — TP = exact ground-truth matches, FP = extraneous mappings, FN = missing mappings, incorrect mapping counts as FP + FN.

**A.3.5 Instruction following (details).** 200 Guardian articles obtained via API; first n sentences as source text; four subtask prompts (Table 12: "Please paraphrase based on the sentences provided," etc.); instruction count sampled uniformly 2-5 then deconflicted, yielding approximately normal distribution with mode 2-3 instructions; keyword max raised from 2 to 5; full meta-prompt template given. Scoring: mean of prompt-level accuracy (1 iff every instruction followed) and instruction-level accuracy (fraction followed), scaled to [0,1].

**A.3.6 Language (details).** Connections: single-turn (original NYT allows retries; to compensate they include smaller puzzles); split = 15 eight-word, 15 twelve-word, 20 sixteen-word questions; score = fraction of groups output correctly; example with ground truth given. Typos: source abstracts first manually de-typo'd; list of common human misspellings assembled from online sources; per-question flip probability p ~ U(0.5, 0.7) applied to every correctly spelled word that has a known misspelling; 50 questions in first release; scored by whether the ground-truth abstract is contained in the LLM output (binary containment). Plot unscrambling: films released after Jan 1, 2024; synopsis lengths 7-66 sentences; transcription errors handled by difflib fuzzy match (Ratcliff/Obershelp) since strongest models exceed 95% transcription accuracy but tend to fix grammar/spelling; score = 1 - d/n_sentences_gt with d = Levenshtein distance between orderings; the authors defend the possibly-below-100% attainable ceiling by analogy to perplexity. Table 13 (task sizes and sources): cta 50, tablejoin 50, tablereformat 50 (Kaggle/Socrata); summarize/paraphrase/story_generation/simplify 50 each (Guardian); typos 50 (arXiv), connections 50 (NYT daily puzzles), plot_unscrambling 40 (IMDb); web_of_lies_v2 50 (synthetic), zebra_puzzle 50 (synthetic), spatial 50 (manually created); olympiad 36 (2024 olympiads), AMPS_Hard 100 (synthetic), math_comp 96 (AMC 2023 + AIME 2024); coding_completion 50, LCB_generation 78 (LiveCodeBench/LeetCode). Total = 1000.

**A.4 Grading methodology.** Regex-based automatic grading. Two acknowledged pitfalls: (i) answer formatting injects an instruction-following component into every task; (ii) format strictness could favor particular LLM families. Mitigations: fully specified, unambiguous answer-format instructions with an in-prompt example; "fully permissive" parsing (rule of thumb: if a human could comprehend the correct answer, mark it correct — e.g., boxed{} or fbox{}, letter or raw multiple-choice answer); flexible regex for different notation styles; regular manual inspection of a random sample of responses labeled incorrect per model to find missed formats; ongoing scoring-function updates; re-evaluation when adding models/tasks.

**A.5 Quality control for new models.** Four-step pipeline: (1) match published chat template/system message/hyperparameters (prefer trusted APIs even for open-weight models because "new models occasionally have bugs when they are first released"); (2) compare new model's per-task scores against similar-scoring models and same-family models to flag anomalously low tasks; (3) manually inspect incorrect answers on flagged tasks plus a random sample across tasks — checks target false negatives; false positives deemed "extremely unlikely" but occasionally audited; (4) update scoring functions if needed, then re-score all models with the updated functions.

**A.6 Question update policy.** Monthly updates replacing 1/6 of questions; newest questions private for one month ("completely contamination-free"); update selection = oldest tasks, easiest tasks, plus any suspected contamination. Table 14: every task except spatial ("No - create new questions by hand") can be updated by script; olympiad and math_comp only yearly (competition release cadence). Key policy: "we always modify the generation script when creating new questions, in order to make sure that the distribution of questions is different and harder over time." After updates, all models are re-run; version control maintained; community contributions welcomed.

**A.7 Contamination in LiveBench.** Distinguishes (1) test-set contamination (test data literally in training data) from (2) task contamination / train-test distribution similarity. Level-(2) generalization (train on AMC 12A 2023, test on AMC 12A 2024) is considered "fair game," but LiveBench still guards against excessive (2): generator code never released, generator always modified at refresh, newest questions withheld a month. Frank admission on (1): some coding questions date to November 2023, and AMC questions received only low-level modification of their November 2023 versions, so "a limited fraction of LiveBench is likely contaminated on all recent LLMs."

**B Additional documentation.** B.1: authors bear responsibility; Apache License 2.0. B.2: hosted on HuggingFace (huggingface.co/livebench); actively maintained. B.3: Contributor Covenant 2.0 code of conduct. B.4: Gebru-style datasheet in repo. B.5 statistics (Table 15, tokens measured with gpt-4-turbo-2024-04-09): 1000 questions total; mean input 1612 tokens/question, mean output 395; extremes — AMPS_Hard input mean 193.86, olympiad input mean 4646.08 and output mean 1221.22, cta output mean 2.42, zebra output mean 616.94. ## Benchmark anatomy

- **N items:** 1000 questions (after first monthly update), 18 tasks in 6 categories. Per-task n: 50 for thirteen tasks, plot_unscrambling 40, olympiad 36, math_comp 96, LCB_generation 78, AMPS_Hard 100. Initial release was smaller (first update added 90 questions to reach 1000); each monthly refresh replaces ~1/6 (~167 questions; the second update replaced 132).
- **Construction method:** two recipes. (a) Recent-information-source tasks: harvest post-cutoff artifacts (Guardian articles via API, Kaggle/Socrata datasets, arXiv abstracts, IMDb/Wikipedia synopses of 2024+ films, NYT Connections, AMC/AIME/SMC/USAMO/IMO 2023-24, LiveCodeBench April 2024 problems) and wrap them in programmatic task templates. (b) Procedurally generated harder variants of known tasks: Web of Lies v2 (parameterized red herrings 0-19, three queried people, location indirection), Zebra (people 3-4, attributes 3-4, constraint level uniform [10,20], solver-verified unique solution), AMPS_Hard (random primitives from widened distributions), olympiad equation-reordering (10/50/80% masking), IFEval-style instruction composition (2-5 instructions, deconflicted).
- **Who authored items:** the author team, mostly via generator scripts operating on external sources; 50 spatial questions are handwritten by the authors; source math problems were authored by competition committees, then modified (prose, answer order, output format).
- **Validation/review:** unique-solution solver for Zebra; manual typo-cleaning of arXiv abstracts before injecting synthetic typos; manual inspection of random samples of incorrect responses per model (false-negative hunting) and occasional false-positive audits; per-model anomaly screening against similar models when onboarding; re-scoring all models after any scoring-function change. No external/independent review of item correctness is described; no inter-annotator process exists (ground truth is programmatic).
- **Human baseline:** none. No human performance is measured anywhere in the paper; difficulty claims rest entirely on model scores (top model 64.7).
- **Contamination defenses:** monthly refresh (1/6 replaced); one-month embargo on newest questions (1/6 always private); post-cutoff information sources; prose/answer-order/format modification of competition questions; generator code never publicly released; generator scripts always modified between refreshes ("different and harder"); selection of easiest/oldest tasks for replacement. Admitted residual: Nov 2023 coding questions and lightly modified AMC 2023 questions "likely contaminated on all recent LLMs."
- **Scoring method:** fully automatic, no LLM judge. Per-question score in [0,1]. Exact/regex match with deliberately permissive parsing (boxed/fbox, letter-or-value MC); Sympy semantic+numeric equivalence for AMPS_Hard; pass@1 against all public+private test cases for coding; edit distance on equation order for olympiad; 1 - Levenshtein/n on sentence order (with difflib fuzzy transcription matching) for plot unscrambling; fraction of correct groups for Connections; binary containment of ground-truth abstract for typos; Accuracy@1 exact match (case-insensitive) for CTA; all-tests-pass for TableReformat; column-level F1 for TableJoin; mean of prompt-level and instruction-level accuracy for instruction following. Aggregation: question -> task mean -> category mean -> LiveBench score = mean of 6 category means (i.e., tasks are implicitly weighted by category, not by question count).
- **Judge design:** none in the benchmark proper. Appendix A.2 contains an anti-judge ablation: MT-Bench-style binary correctness prompt, gpt-4-turbo-2024-04-09 as judge, yielding 10.3-46.0% judge error rates on AMC12/AIME/SMC/Zebra and large score inflation for weak performances (GPT-4-Turbo Zebra 38 GT vs 68 LLM-judged; AIME 13.8 GT vs 35.7 LLM-judged).
- **Statistical reporting:** 95% bootstrap confidence intervals shown in Figures 1 and 5 (bar charts only; no numeric CI values in text or tables); Pearson correlations with standard errors for category/task-vs-overall (Tables 5-6) and best-fit-line residual analysis (Figure 3, Table 7); benchmark-to-benchmark correlations (0.91, 0.88); rank correlation >0.997 across refreshes; mean/median score drift (~-1.2%) across refreshes. No significance tests between adjacent models, no clustered/hierarchical error estimation (bootstrap method and clustering unit are not described), single run per model at temperature 0 (no repeat sampling, no variance over generations).

## Reported results

- Top overall: o1-preview-2024-09-12 = 64.7 (coding 50.8, data analysis 64.0, IF 74.6, language 68.7, math 62.9, reasoning 67.4); described as "6% better than all other models." No model reaches 70%.

- Uncertainty: 95% bootstrap CIs are displayed graphically in Figures 1/5 only; no numeric interval is printed for any score. From Figure 1, top-model CIs visibly overlap for closely ranked models, but the paper never quantifies this.
- Category-vs-overall Pearson r (std err): math 0.9477 (0.0643), reasoning 0.9439 (0.0709), data_analysis 0.9315 (0.0489), coding 0.8970 (0.0840), language 0.8970 (0.0831), instruction_following 0.8174 (0.0811). Task-vs-overall extremes: web_of_lies_v2 0.9136 (0.1454) high; tablereformat 0.6840 (0.1058) low.
- Cross-benchmark: r = 0.91 vs ChatBot Arena; r = 0.88 vs Arena-Hard; GPT-4 models are Arena-Hard outliers (self-judge bias); gemini models are Arena outliers (style preference).
- Refresh stability: rank correlation > 0.997 (original vs update 1; update 1 vs update 2); mean and median score of persistent models dropped about 1.2% as questions were made harder.
- Olympiad subtask (June 2024 run, 34 models): best gpt-4o-2024-05-13 avg 63.85; hardness gradient Easy 57.48 / Medium 29.60 / Hard 19.11; per-question range 96.8 (10% masked, GPT-4o) down to 36 (80% masked).
- LLM-judge ablation: judge error rates — GPT-4-Turbo's answers: AMC12 0.380, AIME 0.214, SMC 0.353, Zebra 0.420; Claude-3-Opus's answers: 0.388, 0.103, 0.294, 0.460. Score distortion: GPT-4-Turbo Zebra 38 (GT) vs 68 (LLM-judged); AIME 13.793 vs 35.714; Claude-3-Opus AMC12 56 vs 42.857 (judging can deflate as well as inflate).
- Cost to run all 1000 questions (Oct 1, 2024 prices): $0.90 (claude-3-haiku) to $53.80 (claude-3-opus); o1-preview $47.87. Mean tokens/question: 1612 in, 395 out (gpt-4-turbo tokenizer).

## Limitations

**Admitted by the authors:**
- English-only; non-English tasks are future work (Section 5).
- Ground-truth scoring cannot evaluate open-ended tasks ("write a travel guide to Hawaii") (Section 5).
- Residual prompt-format biases: "there are still biases due to certain LLM families favoring certain prompt types" (Section 5).
- Incomplete contamination protection: Nov 2023 coding questions and lightly modified AMC 2023 questions mean "a limited fraction of LiveBench is likely contaminated on all recent LLMs" (A.7).
- Automated grading injects an instruction-following component into every task and risks format bias (A.4).
- The LLM-judge ablation "results are not definitive"; a task-tailored judge prompt might do better (Section 3.3, A.2).
- Olympiad results were run in June 2024, before claude-3.5-sonnet existed (A.3.1 footnote), so subtask tables lag the main table's model set.
- Plot-unscrambling may have an attainable ceiling strictly below 100% (defended by the perplexity analogy) (A.3.6).

**Observed by me, not admitted:**
- No human baseline anywhere: "challenging" is defined solely relative to current models; ambiguous or mis-keyed items cannot be distinguished from genuinely hard ones (especially handwritten spatial questions and Connections puzzles, which have known alternative-grouping ambiguity).
- No numeric uncertainty: CIs exist only as unlabeled whiskers in two figures; no interval, standard error, or significance test accompanies any headline claim ("6% better," model orderings). With n=36-100 per task and 40-100-point binomial-ish scores, many adjacent rankings are within noise, and the paper never says which.
- Single run at temperature 0: within-model generation variance is unmeasured; bootstrap over questions captures item sampling only. The bootstrap's resampling unit is never specified (question? task? category?), which matters because the score is a mean of category means, not of questions.
- Aggregation distortion: averaging categories weights an olympiad question (36 per category-sixth) about 4x an AMPS_Hard question; task counts differ 36-100 but the flat category average hides this.
- Internal inconsistency: Section 3.2 claims math_comp correlates highest with overall performance, but Table 6 lists web_of_lies_v2 higher (0.9136 vs 0.9035).
- Contamination defense is procedural, not verified: no membership-inference or cutoff-discontinuity test is run on LiveBench itself; "contamination-limited" is argued from construction, not measured. Monthly "refresh" also means the benchmark is a moving target — scores across versions are not strictly comparable even though the paper compares them (the 1.2% drift conflates question hardness with question identity).
- Task-contamination guard rests on secrecy of generator code that the paper says is always modified, but the *task formats* are fully public, so targeted fine-tuning on look-alike generated data (their own definition (2)) remains easy for 16 of 18 tasks; they acknowledge the definition but claim only that they "attempt to guard against excessive uses."
- Several scoring choices are coarse or fragile: typos is binary substring containment (one residual typo scores 0, and any unicode/whitespace divergence could too); CTA uses the original column header as ground truth, which is frequently non-unique or uninformative in real Kaggle tables (synonym columns make the "wrong" options arguably correct); instruction-following measures only constraint adherence, not output quality, so degenerate compliant text scores 1.
- Anti-saturation plan depends on indefinite maintainer effort and funding (acknowledged as "easily within the computational budgets of the authors' institutions" — an assertion, not a mechanism).
- The 30-70% target band is aspirational: five categories' top scores already exceed 70 for the best models (e.g., IF 80.1), and cta/typos-style tasks sit near ceiling for top models per Table 7 residuals; the paper does not report per-task score distributions.

## Implications for CRUCIBLE-CHAIN

1. **Report cluster-aware bootstrap CIs, and grow independent templates before instances.** LiveBench's only uncertainty display is a 95% bootstrap CI over ~1000 items spread over 18 heterogeneous tasks, and its per-task correlations carry std errors of 0.05-0.15 — single tasks are unreliable measures on 36-100 items. CRUCIBLE-CHAIN's 8 templates x ~18 instances yields ~144 items but only 8 quasi-independent clusters; instances within a template share failure modes, so effective n is nearer 8 than 144. Action: target LiveBench-scale task diversity (~18+ independent template families), and compute CIs by bootstrap over *templates* (or a hierarchical bootstrap: template then instance), not over items — otherwise CIs will be overconfident exactly the way LiveBench's item-level whiskers are. For the question of record: at a true pass rate of 5%, a +/-2.5pp 95% CI needs n≈300 independent-ish items (Wilson), i.e., roughly 18 templates x 18 instances if between-template variance is kept small; separating two frontier models at 3% vs 8% needs ~250+ items per model plus paired-comparison (McNemar-style) testing that LiveBench never does but CRUCIBLE-CHAIN can, since all models see identical items.
2. **Treat the generator and prompt scaffolding as private, embargoed assets.** LiveBench's central defenses map exactly onto the failure that saturated CRUCIBLE-CHAIN: they never release generator code, they *always* modify the generator between refreshes so the distribution gets harder, and they keep 1/6 of questions private on a one-month embargo. CRUCIBLE-CHAIN's prompts leaked "method recipes and answer menus" — the equivalent of shipping the generator inside every item. Action: strip all method/menu scaffolding from prompts (present raw data + question only, never the option list the generator used); publish only spent instances; keep a never-published held-out instance set per template, regenerated with fresh seeds per evaluation run; and version the generator so each release provably differs.
3. **Build difficulty dials into every template and re-tune to a target band each release.** LiveBench parameterizes hardness (olympiad masking 10/50/80% -> scores 96.8 -> 36; zebra constraint levels 10-20; red herrings uniform 0-19; typo rate U(0.5,0.7)) and refreshes "the currently easiest tasks" first, keeping top models in a 30-70% band with a measured -1.2% drift per refresh cycle. CRUCIBLE-CHAIN targets single-digit pass rates: give each template explicit knobs (subtlety of the planted defect, semantic distance between correct path and attractive wrong path, chain length 5->8, red-herring count in provided data) and calibrate knob settings against 2-3 frontier models per release, instead of hand-authoring a new template whenever one saturates. LiveBench proves a generator-modification cadence can outrun frontier progress for at least several refresh cycles with rank correlation >0.997.
4. **Decouple parse failure from reasoning failure — non-compensatory scoring multiplies the confound LiveBench documents.** LiveBench admits regex grading "adds an instruction-following element" to every task, and mitigates with maximally permissive parsers plus routine manual audits of a random sample of *incorrect-labeled* responses per model, updating parsers and re-scoring everyone when a new format appears. In CRUCIBLE-CHAIN, one unparsed stage zeroes a 5-8 stage chain, so a 2% per-stage parse-failure rate fabricates a 10-15% chain failure rate — fatal when the signal itself is single-digit. Action: per-stage permissive extraction with several accepted formats; log parse-failure separately from wrong-answer at every stage; audit a random sample of zero-scored chains per model per release (LiveBench's exact QC loop, A.5); report the parse-failure rate alongside pass rate so auditors can bound the artifact.
5. **Never fall back to LLM judging — including for the flawed-premise/refusal condition.** LiveBench measured judge error rates of 10.3-46.0% on precisely the hard math/reasoning items where judging is most tempting, with distortion in both directions (Zebra 38 GT -> 68 judged; AMC12 56 GT -> 42.9 judged). At CRUCIBLE-CHAIN's target of single-digit pass rates, a judge inflating weak performance the way GPT-4-Turbo inflated Zebra (+30 points) would destroy the benchmark's headline claim. The refusal-is-correct condition is the danger zone: "did the model refuse for the right reason" invites an LLM judge. Action: score refusal deterministically (structured response field: proceed/flag-premise + which premise, matched against generator metadata), mirroring how LiveBench turned unjudgeable proofs into a deterministic reordering task rather than judging proof text.
6. **Adopt LiveBench's refresh-regression metrics to certify the post-leak regeneration.** LiveBench validates each refresh with (a) rank correlation of model scores across versions (>0.997) and (b) mean-score drift (-1.2%). CRUCIBLE-CHAIN just regenerated after a leak: run the pre-leak model set on the regenerated suite and report the same two numbers. If ranks are preserved while absolute scores fall from 94-100% to the target band, that is evidence the fix removed leakage rather than changed the construct; publish this table in the release notes as LiveBench does in Section 3.4.
7. **Exploit the one advantage LiveBench lacks: free instances enable repeats and a measurable ceiling.** LiveBench is rate-limited by the real world (competitions are yearly; Table 14 marks olympiad/math_comp "can only be updated yearly" and spatial "create new questions by hand") and so runs each model once at temperature 0, leaving generation variance unmeasured; it also has *no human baseline*, so "hard" is defined only against models. CRUCIBLE-CHAIN's deterministic generator mints unlimited instances at near-zero label error: (a) run k=3-5 seeds/repeats per template-condition cell to estimate within-model variance that LiveBench cannot, and fold it into the CI; (b) run a small expert-human baseline on a sample of chains — with constructed truth, human pass rate is cheap to score and anchors the claim that single-digit model scores reflect difficulty, not ambiguity; (c) budget like LiveBench documents ($0.90-$53.80 per 1000 single-turn questions; o1-preview $47.87) — a 3-condition x 18-template x 18-instance x 5-repeat campaign is ~4,860 chains x 5-8 stages, so pre-compute token cost per model the way Table 15/16 does before committing to scale.
8. **Report per-condition and per-template scores with the aggregation rule stated up front.** LiveBench's headline number is a mean of category means, silently weighting an olympiad question ~4x an AMPS_Hard question, and the paper contains an internal inconsistency (Section 3.2 vs Table 6) about which task best tracks the average. CRUCIBLE-CHAIN's three conditions (clean/planted/flawed) are structurally different measurements — averaging them into one number would bury the refusal-condition signal exactly the way LiveBench's category averaging buries task imbalance. Action: define the headline metric now (e.g., minimum across conditions, or report the triple), weight templates equally with instance counts documented, and machine-check every in-text numeric claim against the tables before release.
