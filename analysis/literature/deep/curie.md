## Section-by-section notes

**Abstract.** Introduces CURIE: 10 tasks, 580 problem–solution pairs curated by experts in six disciplines (materials science, condensed matter physics, quantum computing, geospatial analysis, biodiversity, proteins), covering experimental and theoretical workflows. Evaluates closed and open LLMs; Gemini Flash 2.0 and Claude-3 show consistent high comprehension; GPT-4o and Command-R+ "fail dramatically on protein sequencing tasks." Headline: best performance 32%.

**Sec. 1 Introduction.** Motivation: existing science benchmarks (MMLU, GPQA, PubmedQA) are short-question, mostly multiple-choice; context windows have grown 8k → 1M+, but no benchmark tests scientific long-context reasoning. CURIE: 10 tasks, 580 pairs, 429 research documents, six disciplines. Evaluates 8 SOTA models with ≥32k context. Materials tasks (exhaustive retrieval + aggregation) exceptionally hard for all models. GPT-4o pathology on PDB: fails to stop generation, repeats subsequences. Gemini Flash 2.0 wrote correct Python for PDB about half the time. Authors also pitch the annotations as a resource for planning/instruction-following/mixed-format evaluation research.

**Sec. 2 Related work.** Three strands: (i) science NLP tasks (BLURB, NER, PICO, relation extraction, IE, QA) — all short inputs (abstracts, sentences); (ii) long-context benchmarks (ZeroScrolls, NIAH, LongBench, Bamboo, M4LE, L-eval, Loogle; synthetic length-adaptable Ruler, Ada-Leval) — none scientific; (iii) scientific-literature QA (QASA, QASPER) — full papers but QA only, because expert task collection is expensive. CURIE: 10 tasks, all annotated by PhD-holding experts, average input ~15k words (Fig. 2c), outputs average ~1k words (actually 954, Fig. 4c).

**Sec. 3 CURIE dataset and tasks.** Design criteria per task: (1) realistic expert task, (2) answer information contained in the provided context, (3) expert-evaluable with metrics. Seven assessment categories: concept extraction, concept tracking (co-reference), aggregation, algebraic manipulation, summarization, visual comprehension, cross-domain expertise. Worked with 1–3 experts per domain on paper sourcing, ground-truth creation, and metric identification; experts also rated per-example difficulty. Papers restricted to permissive licenses. Task inventory:
- **DFT** (materials): 3 subtasks — DFT-S structures, DFT-P parameters, DFT-C code. "Two Ph.Ds with expertise in DFT computations identified 75 papers" (Table 3 and App. F.1 say 74 — internal inconsistency).
- **MPV** (materials): 17 papers; exhaustively extract material/descriptor/property/value + source passages; ablations for specific properties (refractive index, band gap) and non-trivial properties.
- **HFD/HFE** (condensed matter): HFD = derive Hartree-Fock mean-field Hamiltonian, 13–19 reasoning steps, template with placeholders (~6k-word prompt); HFE = extract most general mean-field Hamiltonian. 53 papers (38 HFE, 15 HFD).
- **QECC** (quantum computing): produce an Error Correction Zoo YAML entry from a paper; 65 papers, curated by experienced EC Zoo experts.
- **GEO** (geospatial): 19 papers; extract all datasets with sources, variables, time ranges, spatial ranges.
- **BIOGR** (biodiversity, multimodal): 138 map images + captions from ecology papers; predict lat/long bounding box; ground truth expert-verified.
- **PDB** (proteins): 64 structures; reconstruct amino-acid sequence from 3D coordinates stripped of functional annotation.
Annotations include ground truth + per-example difficulty. Cross-review on extraction tasks (MPV, HFE, GEO, DFT-S) "reported a high rate of agreement."

**Sec. 4.1 Experimental setup.** Models: closed — GPT-4o, Claude-3 Opus, Gemini 1.0 Pro, 1.5 Pro, 1.5 Flash, 2.0 Flash; open — Mixtral (8x7b), Command-R+, LongLLaMa-3B. Zero-shot standard template: task description + output format, then full paper (LongLLaMa gets paper first). DFT and MPV prompts include a hand-crafted excerpt example to clarify format. BIOGR input is image+caption only. Critically: "Performance is reported for each model on each task using a single run." (BIOGR is an exception: 3 runs, per App. F.6.3.)

**Sec. 4.2 Evaluation metrics.** ROUGE-L + BERTScore-F1 for the 8 free-text tasks; BIOGR: IoU on lat/long boxes (plus normalized distance error and relative box size in App. Table 7); PDB: identity ratio (IDr) = identities / alignment length from Biopython pairwise alignment. Primary per-task metric for averaging (App. Table 4): LLMSim-F1 for DFT-S, DFT-P, MPV; ROUGE-L for DFT-C, HFD, HFE, QECC, GEO; IoU for BIOGR; IDr for PDB; all normalized to [0,1]. Human evaluations: experts rated 10–12% of responses per task good/ok/bad.

**Sec. 4.3 Model-based evaluation.** Free-form mixed-format outputs (e.g., grid points as [p,q,r] vs p×q×r) defeat exact metrics; MCQ would forfeit expressiveness. Two proposals: **LLMSim** — a CoT LLM judge matches each ground-truth dictionary to the most similar predicted dictionary (or None), enabling precision/recall/F1 over retrieved items; **LMScore** (App. D) — weighted 3-point score from LLM log-probs over {bad, ok, good}, weights {0, 0.5, 1}.

**Sec. 5 Results.** Claude-3 Opus best average with consistent performance; GPT-4o best on GEO and BIOGR (contradicted by Table 1 IoU: Gemini 2.0 Flash 0.49 > Gemini 1.5 Pro 0.42 > Claude 0.39 > GPT-4o 0.37) but very low on PDB (repetition collapse) and HFD (formatting: emitted literal "key"/"value" tokens). "Overall though, most of the closed models had similar performance, and given the variability (e.g., 25%-75% error bars around the mean in Fig. 5) the difference between them is not significant." Fig. 1(b): generation-over-generation gains on MMLU/DROP/GPQA have not transferred to CURIE. Table 1 (headline automated metrics, selected): HFE ROUGE-L — Gemini 2.0 Flash 50.06, Claude 49.10, GPT-4o 48.93, Mixtral 9.15; GEO R-L — 2.0 Flash 29.69, Claude 28.66, GPT-4o 27.30; QECC R-L — 1.5 Flash 23.50, 2.0 Flash 22.36, Claude 21.81, GPT-4o 20.02; HFD R-L — 1.5 Flash 22.86, 2.0 Flash 22.52, GPT-4o 9.81; MPV R-L — 1.5 Pro 31.54 (outlier), Claude 15.86; DFT avg R-L ~11–14 all models; BIOGR IoU 0.34–0.49 (closed; open models 0.00, no multimodal support); PDB IDr — 2.0 Flash 0.56, 1.5 Pro 0.29, Claude 0.29, GPT-4o 0.13, LongLLaMa 0.00. Human-vs-model eval: LLMSim precision/recall "closely match" expert-measured values (checked on Gemini 1.5 Pro and GPT-4o only). Human 3-point ratings correlate with LMScore (App. Fig. 10) but "it requires further analysis prior to wider usage." Difficulty slicing (Fig. 7): models substantially better on easy; medium ≈ hard; more medium than hard examples exist. Difficulty was mostly operationalized as information dispersion; for DFT-C the implementable-function ratio; HFD reasoning-step count; GEO dataset count; PDB sequence length. Table 2 (LLMSim P/R/F1, selected F1): DFT-S — 1.5 Flash 35.28, Claude 33.76, 1.5 Pro 32.11, Command-R+ 32.19, GPT-4o 30.63, LongLLaMa 1.36; DFT-P — GPT-4o 22.13, Gemini 1.0 Pro 20.56, Claude 19.87, 2.0 Flash 7.68; MPV — 2.0 Flash 36.99, Claude 34.04, GPT-4o 25.90; MPV-non-trivial — Claude 38.32 (precision jumps: GPT-4o 45.10, Claude 45.64); MPV-specific — Claude 31.48.

**Sec. 6 Discussion.** (1) Robustness: "there is variability across model runs, even though average performance remained fairly constant" — higher on harder tasks; instruction-following failures persist despite in-prompt examples; models sometimes refuse to guess even when told to. Model-based metrics mitigated format noncompliance. (2) Retrieval: HFE (single-needle variant) has high scores; exhaustive multi-value retrieval + aggregation (DFT, MPV, GEO) much lower. (3) Concept tracking/aggregation: Claude-3 groups DFT parameters by purpose (names a set "DFT-parameters-convergence-tests"); QECC summaries succinct with many key "nuggets" — experts note excluding wrong bits is easier than combing them out of the paper; GEO degrades for multi-dataset, multi-continent extents. (4) Closed vs open: Command-R+ (retrieval-augmented) near closed-weight on QECC/DFT/MPV; open models lack multimodal; Mixtral beat GPT-4o on PDB. (5) Reasoning in code: Gemini 2.0 Flash wrote a correct, executable Python function for PDB about half the time.

**Sec. 7 Conclusion.** Contributions: (i) 580-example benchmark from 429 papers, six disciplines; (ii) 10 realistic multi-capability tasks; (iii) model-based metrics (LLMSim, LMScore) validated against human evaluation; (iv) curation guidelines for multi-step tasks with complex answers.

**App. D LMScore.** LMScore = Σ p(x_t)·w_t over tokens {bad, ok, good}, weights {0, 0.5, 1}; probabilities from softmax over the three tokens' log-probs (GPT-4o as judge; uncased; 'ok'≡'okay'; approximation when tokens absent from top-5 log-probs). Fig. 10: correlation with human 3-point ratings across ROUGE-scored tasks (no correlation coefficient reported). Table 5 (R-L vs LMS per task) shows similar trends to ROUGE-L; e.g., HFE LMS: Claude 0.50, GPT-4o 0.47 vs HFD LMS: Claude 0.13, GPT-4o 0.06. **App. E Limitations (authors').** (1) Scale limited by the cost of high-quality expert annotation. (2) ROUGE-L/BERTScore "can be unforgiving" to reasonable-but-different responses; model-based metrics "still far from perfect." (3) Only zero-/two-shot evaluated; RAG and chained prompting left to future work. (4) No human baseline: no single person has cross-domain expertise, and hiring per-domain experts "would double the cost of annotation," so they "rely on annotator adjudication to come up with the final answers."

**App. F.1 DFT.** Motivation: DFT requires PhD-level training; benchmark is a step toward automating DFT workflows. Collection: ~200 S2ORC papers mentioning DFT in abstract → two hired PhD annotators (Materials Science, DFT practitioners) selected 74 papers (excluded method-development and theory-only papers). Annotation: identify structures; build computational-step graph with inputs/outputs; topologically sort into a computational graph; implement each function in Python (ASE library); placeholders where the paper lacks information; final executable script including analysis. Full prompts reproduced (Figs. 11–13); DFT-S requires a list of dicts with 18 named fields; DFT-P a JSON per calculation with 12+ fields; DFT-C modular Python. Results: Claude-3 Opus best at counting distinct structures (specific record IDs cited); on DFT-P Claude names parameter sets by purpose but its brevity sometimes misses a second parameter set that Gemini 1.5 Flash finds; on DFT-C, Gemini 1.5 Flash emits stub functions ("return 0"), Mixtral fails to finish; Claude-3 best overall.

**App. F.2 MPV.** Motivation: unstructured literature as untapped materials database; rule-based extraction misses context. Collection: 17 S2ORC papers; hired experts extracted all material names, descriptors, properties, property descriptors, values, plus source sentences. Three task variants: all properties (main), specific (refractive index + bandgap), non-trivial. Difficulty rubric: easy = answer in one sentence unmodified; medium = descriptors essential; hard = multi-sentence/table/figure reasoning. Evaluation: match on the 4-tuple (material, material descriptor, property, value) with semantic equivalence ("TiO2 nanocrystal" = "TiO2" + descriptor "nanocrystal"); LLM judge instructed toward leniency (InN vs Indium Nitride; unit-order differences); source passages excluded from matching. Findings: models extract trivial properties (e.g., sample thickness) humans omit; non-trivial prompt filter significantly improves precision at similar recall; specific-property filter raises precision further but lowers recall. (Text cites "Table 3" for these ablations; they are actually in Table 2 — minor erratum.)

**App. F.3 Hartree-Fock tasks.** HFD: template generalizes Pan et al. (2024); 15 papers hand-selected by expert post-docs; consistent format enables automated eval; ground truth includes all derivations per reasoning step; prompt (~6k words) instructs a pre-derivation system choice (continuum single-particle / continuum second-quantized / lattice) and stepwise placeholder filling with JSON output. HFE: arXiv advanced search (cond-mat, cross-list on, "Hartree-Fock" in abstract), reverse chronological, ~50–80 candidates filtered by an expert doctoral candidate to 38 papers with explicit HF Hamiltonians; annotator extracted and aggregated equations. Difficulty rubrics given for both. Results: HFD hard for all — Gemini 1.5 Pro best; GPT-4o and LongLLaMa worst; GPT-4o hurt by JSON formatting ("key"/"value" artifacts). HFE: all models except LongLLaMa consistently good; gap between HFE and HFD even clearer under LMScore and human eval.

**App. F.4 QECC.** EC Zoo maintained manually by a "Zookeeper." 65 arXiv papers, 1997–2024, curated by two experts with prior EC Zoo experience; composition enumerated: 15 quantum-matter-phase codes, 14 continuous-variable, 12 high-performing qubit codes, 7 general qubit families, 4 general qudit, 4 classical, 2 fermionic, 2 randomized constructions, 2 classical-over-quantum-channel, 2 magic-state distillation, 1 holography. Evaluated by ROUGE; a sample of ~20 outputs carefully read by a human expert. Findings: LLMs summarize well, respect YAML format, are consistent across disciplines; sometimes flag correct "nuggets" *missing from the ground truth* (buried numerical parameters; an independent-discovery citation); technical detail level low, pedagogy rare (a Claude-3 exception reproduced, with LaTeX subsections for LDPC code constructions); much output is "extraneous information that ranged from superficial 'fluff' to complete hallucination," though easy for experts to spot; verdict — LLM drafts make EC Zoo curation faster because verification is quicker than identification. Worked example (Menicucci 2014 GKP cluster-state paper) shows correct extraction of 20.5 dB threshold, 12.6% distillation error probability, and both code relations.

**App. F.5 GEO.** 19 papers across earth observation, economics, epidemiology, public health from DigitalCommons/ScienceDirect/MDPI/NIH/arXiv, open licenses only (started larger, shrank due to licensing). Deliberately varied difficulty: compound time ranges ("Jan-Apr for every year from 2012-2015"), mixed spatial ranges, information scattered through intro/figures, and some papers missing key info entirely — annotators used domain expertise to make educated guesses and documented shortcomings in notes; two annotators, verified by PhD experts; format design itself was iterative and challenging. Difficulty rubric based on dispersion. Results: models often had right pieces in wrong format or unparsable JSON; programmatic metrics struggle (exact match for time ranges vs semantic match for descriptions; muddled variable-vs-dataset naming); LMScore worked best and correlated strongly with human evaluation for this task. Closed-model ROUGE-L clustered in [28, 32]; open in the 20s, LongLLaMa <10. Slight, inconsistent inverse correlation of score with number of datasets (Fig. 26); multi-continent spatial extents markedly worse (Fig. 27); Europe/Antarctica better scoped.

**App. F.6 BIOGR.** Sources: ecology articles (bioRxiv, ScienceDirect, Nature), open licenses; deliberately varied map features (insets, displayed coordinates, recognizable shapes, repeated instances). Deep-analysis set: 24 map images from 24 papers, expert ground truth, 1–10 difficulty scale mapped to easy/medium/hard; a further 114 examples with metadata on GitHub (total 138). Prompt explicitly requests a guess: "If you are not sure, please guess at an answer anyway. I'd rather have an answer than no answer at all." All models complied with JSON format (after filtering preambles). Results over 3 model runs: text claims average IoU per model "ranged between 0.42 and 0.54 (Table 1)" — but Table 1 shows 0.34–0.49 and Table 7 shows Claude 0.39 / Gemini 1.5 Pro 0.42 / 2.0 Flash 0.49 / 1.5 Flash 0.36 / GPT-4o 0.37 / 1.0 Pro 0.34 (another internal inconsistency); normalized distance error: GPT-4o best 1.43, 2.0 Flash 3.03, Gemini 1.5 Pro worst 14.28; relative box size: 1.5 Pro 1.20 best, 2.0 Flash 3.05 worst. Refusals occurred despite the guess instruction; lowering temperature reduced variability but *increased* refusals. Ablations (Gemini 1.5 Pro): image-only ≈ full input; caption-only much worse with more non-responses. Subset analyses: coordinates-on-map better; larger regions much easier (overlap chance + recognizable shapes); small-regions-without-insets beat with-insets — confounded because that subset had displayed coordinates or famous cities; authors suspect models ignore insets. Difficulty ratings correlate with model IoU (GPT-4o cleanest trend). Out-of-scope examples discarded (multiple unrelated maps, non-contiguous crops), acknowledged as a real-world gap. Future: polygons, points of interest, QA pairs, geospatial tool use ("agentic approach").

**App. F.7 PDB.** 64 single-chain proteins spanning 15 species, varied lengths and functions, selected by a domain expert; ground truth sequences from the data bank (also computable programmatically). Task: PDB text → FASTA single-letter sequence; "X" for unknown residues. Evaluation: Biopython pairwise alignment; identity ratio primary; no model-based eval (exact evaluation is straightforward; LLMs are poor at judging sequence similarity; ROUGE unsuitable). Failure modes: context overflow; incomplete traversal (especially large proteins); undercounting consecutive identical residues; using the first letter of the three-letter code instead of the correct three-to-one mapping. IDr drops with sequence length; high variability at short lengths (Fig. 35).

## Benchmark anatomy

- **N items**: 580 claimed (Table 3 itemization sums to 578): DFT-S 74, DFT-P 74, DFT-C 74, MPV 17, HFD 15, HFE 38, QECC 65, GEO 19, BIOGR 138, PDB 64 — from 429 source documents, 10 tasks, 6 domains. Average input ~15k words (max: PDB 44,028); average ground-truth output 954 words.
- **Construction**: real published papers with permissive licenses; tasks defined with 1–3 domain experts per domain to mirror realistic workflows; papers sourced from S2ORC (DFT ~200→74; MPV 17), arXiv advanced search (HFE ~50–80→38), Pan et al. 2024 (HFD 15), EC Zoo expert selection (QECC 65), mixed repositories with license filtering (GEO 19, BIOGR), Protein Data Bank (PDB 64).
- **Item authors**: hired/collaborating experts — all with PhDs or doctoral candidates in the specific field (two DFT PhDs; expert post-docs for HFD; doctoral candidate for HFE; two experienced EC Zoo curators; ecology domain experts; materials experts for MPV; two GEO annotators verified by PhDs).
- **Validation/review**: iterative format design → pilot phase → adjudication → cross-review of each other's annotations. Reported agreement: HFE and DFT-S >95%; MPV >80% (after two feedback rounds); GEO >90%. No agreement numbers for HFD, QECC, DFT-P/C, BIOGR, PDB (PDB ground truth is programmatic).
- **Human baseline**: none. Explicitly declared infeasible (no cross-domain human exists; per-domain experts would double annotation cost). Difficulty ratings and 10–12% response audits are the only human reference points.
- **Contamination defenses**: none discussed. All inputs are public papers; worse, several ground truths are themselves public: QECC targets are real EC Zoo YAML entries in a public GitHub repo; PDB target sequences are in the public Protein Data Bank keyed to the same structures; HFD derivations were published in Pan et al. (2024). The word "contamination" does not appear.
- **Scoring**: mixed per task — ROUGE-L (5 tasks' primary), LLMSim-F1 (3 tasks), IoU (1), identity ratio (1); BERTScore-F1 secondary; LMScore proposed as coarse alternative. All normalized [0,1] and averaged across tasks for the headline number.
- **Judge design**: LLMSim — CoT prompt matching predicted dictionaries to each ground-truth dictionary field-by-field, then P/R/F1 over matches; leniency explicitly instructed (synonyms, unit formats). LMScore — GPT-4o log-prob-weighted 3-point scale (weights 0/0.5/1), with top-5 log-prob approximation fallback. Judge validation: LLMSim P/R "closely match" human expert measurements, but checked on only two models (Gemini 1.5 Pro, GPT-4o); LMScore correlates with expert good/ok/bad ratings on 10–12% samples (Fig. 10; no coefficient reported); authors caution LMScore "requires further analysis prior to wider usage."
- **Statistical reporting**: single run per model per task (BIOGR: 3 runs); no confidence intervals anywhere; no significance tests; no clustering by paper; Fig. 5 shows medians with 25–75% inter-example bars; authors verbally concede closed-model differences are "not significant" given that spread. No cost/compute reporting.

## Limitations

**Admitted by the authors (App. E and scattered):** limited scale due to expert-annotation cost; ROUGE/BERTScore unforgiving to legitimate variation; model-based metrics imperfect and LMScore not ready for wider usage; only zero-/two-shot settings; no human performance baseline (cross-domain expertise doesn't exist in one person; per-domain expert eval would double cost); GEO/BIOGR sample-size caveats for subset analyses ("worth verifying with a large sampling of papers"; inset-subset confound); BIOGR out-of-scope examples discarded.

**Observed by me, not admitted:**
1. **Single-run evaluation contradicts their own variance finding.** They report single runs per task (Sec. 4.1) while the Discussion and F.5.4/F.6.3 repeatedly note run-to-run variability "usually higher on harder tasks." No repeats (except BIOGR's 3), no seeds, no CIs — the headline model ranking is statistically unsupported by their own admission.
2. **Zero contamination defense.** Ground truths for QECC (public EC Zoo repo), PDB (public data bank sequences), and HFD (published Pan et al. 2024 derivations) are plausibly in training data; source papers certainly are. Scores may partly measure memorization, and future models can trivially train on the released ground truth.
3. **Lexical metrics for non-lexical correctness.** ROUGE-L is the primary metric for LaTeX equations (HFE), YAML (QECC), and Python code (DFT-C). A wrong Hamiltonian with the right symbols scores high; correct code with different structure scores low. The HFE ~50 "high performance" claim is weakly grounded.
4. **Internal inconsistencies.** 580 stated vs 578 itemized; 75 vs 74 DFT papers; BIOGR IoU "0.42–0.54" in text vs 0.34–0.49 in Table 1 vs Table 7's values; "GPT-4o outperforms the others on ... BIOGR" vs Table 1 where Gemini 2.0 Flash leads by 0.12 IoU; Table 5 vs Table 1 ROUGE-L discrepancies for identical model/task cells. Sloppy bookkeeping for a benchmark paper.
5. **Judge validated thinly and with conflict of interest.** LLMSim's human agreement checked on 2 of 9 models; LMScore uses GPT-4o to judge a pool that includes GPT-4o; no inter-judge or judge-vs-judge reliability reported, no correlation coefficients given.
6. **Heterogeneous-metric averaging.** The headline "32%" averages ROUGE-L, F1, IoU, and identity ratio — quantities with different chance floors and ceilings; the composite has no clear interpretation, and no chance/trivial-baseline is reported (e.g., IoU of a whole-continent guess, ROUGE-L of copying the abstract).
7. **Difficulty ratings are annotator-internal.** The same experts who wrote the ground truth rated difficulty; medium≈hard model performance suggests the scale doesn't discriminate at the top.
8. **Task-size imbalance.** BIOGR (138) and DFT (3×74) dominate the 580 count while HFD — the only true multi-step reasoning task — has 15 items; the benchmark's "reasoning" claim rests on its thinnest slice.

## Implications for CRUCIBLE-CHAIN

1. **Recipe leakage is survivable only when execution is the bottleneck — CURIE proves both halves.** HFD hands the model the complete 13–19-step recipe as a template with placeholders, yet remains CURIE's hardest task (best R-L 22.86, LMScore ≤0.21) because each step demands symbolic derivation the model must execute itself. Meanwhile HFE-style tasks, where the answer can be pattern-matched from context, sit at R-L ~50. CRUCIBLE-CHAIN's saturation came from leaking both recipe *and* answer menus. Actionable: rebuild prompts to state only the scientific goal (like DFT-C: "reproduce the calculations," no step list, no menu); if stage structure must be exposed for scoring, make each stage's answer a free computation over instance data (numbers, derived quantities) that no menu can contain, and verify per-template that a recipe-following script without judgment still fails the planted-defect condition.

2. **The scale question of record: CURIE is the cautionary n.** With per-task n of 15–74 items, one run, and no clustering, CURIE's authors themselves concede model differences are "not significant" given 25–75% spread. CRUCIBLE-CHAIN's 8 templates × ~18 instances has the same structural weakness: with non-compensatory binary scoring and strong within-template correlation, the effective sample size is closer to 8 than 144. Actionable targets: (a) grow independent templates to ≥25–30 (CURIE's 10 tasks across 6 domains is the floor for cross-capability claims, and its thinnest task, HFD n=15, is exactly the size at which its rankings collapse); (b) ≥3–5 repeats per item — CURIE saw run variance concentrate on hard items, i.e., precisely in the single-digit-pass-rate regime you target, where one run of a 5% process is uninformative; (c) report cluster-robust (template-level bootstrap) CIs and pre-register the model-comparison test; at single-digit pass rates, detecting a 5-point model gap needs on the order of several hundred scored chains per model, so plan items × repeats accordingly.

3. **Constructed truth eliminates CURIE's entire evaluation stack — advertise and protect that.** CURIE needed two novel LLM-judge metrics, 10–12% human audits, leniency instructions, and still concedes LMScore "requires further analysis prior to wider usage"; its GEO evaluation "most success" was an LLM judge because programmatic metrics choked on format variance. CRUCIBLE-CHAIN's deterministic generator makes scoring exact — but only if answer formats are machine-parseable without semantic matching. Actionable: keep every stage answer in a rigid typed schema (number + unit + tolerance, enum, boolean-with-named-defect), tolerate format noise with a deterministic normalizer rather than an LLM matcher, and report the "unparsable answer" rate separately (CURIE folded formatting failures into task scores — GPT-4o's HFD score was destroyed by JSON artifacts, conflating capability with compliance).

4. **Contamination: CURIE's unforced error is CRUCIBLE's structural advantage — if instances rotate.** CURIE's ground truths (EC Zoo YAMLs, PDB sequences, published derivations) are public and memorizable; nothing prevents a model from having trained on them, and the paper never discusses it. CRUCIBLE-CHAIN answers are computed from generated data, so labels can't pre-exist — but leaked *instances* (the 8×18 now in frontier-lab logs after the saturated campaign) are burned. Actionable: treat every released/evaluated instance as contaminated; regenerate fresh instances per evaluation campaign from held-back seeds; keep the generator's parameter distributions private; and add a canary check (re-run a burned instance vs a fresh sibling — a gap flags memorization).

5. **Refusal has a base rate that CURIE measured by accident — your flawed-premise condition must control for it.** CURIE told models to always guess (BIOGR: "I'd rather have an answer than no answer at all") and still got refusals; lowering temperature *increased* refusals while reducing variance. So refusal frequency is a decoding- and instruction-sensitive trait, not pure defect detection. Actionable: score the flawed-premise condition as refusal-with-correct-diagnosis (name the flawed assumption) rather than bare refusal; measure false-refusal rate on clean controls at the same temperature and report the discrimination margin (e.g., d′ between conditions); sweep or fix temperature explicitly, since it shifts the refusal operating point.

6. **Exhaustive-aggregation stages are the durable hardness CURIE found — mine them for new templates.** CURIE's monotonic hardness ordering: single-needle extraction (HFE ~50 R-L) ≫ exhaustive multi-item retrieval + aggregation (DFT-S/P, MPV, GEO: F1 ~7–37), with recall the binding constraint and performance degrading with item count and dispersion (GEO multi-continent worse; MPV recall < precision after filtering). Completeness cannot be faked from a leaked menu because the item *set* varies per instance. Actionable: add chain stages of the form "identify ALL X in the generated dataset" (all defective sensors, all confounded batches, all parameter sets) where downstream stages consume the found set, so a single miss propagates to failure under non-compensatory scoring — this compounds difficulty naturally toward single-digit pass rates.

7. **Difficulty calibration must be empirical, not declared.** CURIE's expert easy/medium/hard ratings (based mostly on information dispersion) produced easy ≫ medium ≈ hard — the top of the scale didn't discriminate, and hard examples were scarce. CRUCIBLE's analogue (stage count, wrong-path attractiveness) should be validated against measured pass rates per instance: after each campaign, fit pass rate vs. designed difficulty; retire or redesign difficulty knobs that don't move the curve; deliberately oversample the empirically-hard tail rather than trusting the design label.

8. **Report per-cell, not composite.** CURIE's "best performance at 32%" averages incommensurable metrics over unbalanced tasks (BIOGR's 138 easy-ish multimodal items vs HFD's 15 reasoning items), and its prose contradicts its own tables in at least four places. CRUCIBLE-CHAIN should publish the full condition × template × model pass-rate matrix with exact binomial or template-bootstrap CIs, keep the headline as all-stages-correct pass rate per condition (never pooled across conditions, since refusal-correct and answer-correct are different constructs), and machine-generate all tables from one results store so text and tables cannot diverge.
