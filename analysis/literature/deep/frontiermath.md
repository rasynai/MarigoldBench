## Section-by-section notes

**Title/authors/affiliations (lines 1-21).** ~40 named authors; Epoch AI core team plus contributing mathematicians flagged with a dagger/double-dagger system ("Core contributor" / "Contributing mathematician"). Institutions include MIT, UC Berkeley, Harvard, Cornell, ETH Zurich, King's College London, and a dozen others. Evaluation access is by request to math_evals@epochai.org. The acknowledgments thank Tao, Gowers, Borcherds for interviews, Tao for contributing several problems, and state: "We gratefully acknowledge OpenAI for their support in creating the benchmark." This is v7, 23 Dec 2025.

**Abstract (lines 22-32).** "A benchmark of hundreds of original, exceptionally challenging mathematics problems crafted and vetted by expert mathematicians." Covers most major branches of modern math. Typical problem takes a researcher in the relevant branch multiple hours; upper end multiple days. New, unpublished problems + automated verification to minimize contamination. Headline: "Current state-of-the-art AI models solve under 2% of problems."

**Section 1: Introduction (lines 33-140).** Two motivating failures of existing benchmarks: (a) saturation — MATH and GSM8K are high-school/early-undergrad and near-perfect for SOTA models; (b) contamination — fresh competition problems (IMO, AIME) avoid it but are few and hard to grade. Figure 1 shows three released sample problems with answers: Artin primitive-root density (answer 367707), degree-19 polynomial p(19) (answer 1876572071974094803391179), p-adic recurrence extension (answer 9811). Figure 2: FrontierMath keeps a >98% unsolved rate vs. Omni-Math, MathVista, AIME, MATH, GSM-8k, MMLU (all far more saturated). Footnote 2: problems span 70% of top-level MSC2020 subjects (excluding General topics, History, Math education). Interviews with Tao, Gowers, Borcherds (Fields Medalists) and Evan Chen: unanimously "exceptionally challenging," though the numerical-answer format "differs from typical mathematical research practice."

**Section 2.1: The collection pipeline (lines 141-183).** 60+ mathematicians, more than a dozen countries, graduate students to faculty, collectively 14 IMO gold medals, one contributor holds a Fields Medal. Four problem-creation requirements:
- **Originality**: novel adaptations or combinations that "obscure their origins" so pattern matching fails.
- **Automated verifiability**: definitive computable answers — integers preferred, or arbitrary SymPy objects (symbolic expressions, matrices, sets).
- **Guessproofness**: rule of thumb that guessing without doing the work must have <1% success probability; answers are large non-obvious numbers.
- **Computational tractability**: author solution scripts must cumulatively run under one minute on standard hardware, starting from standard field knowledge.
Each submission carries a detailed solution, a verification script, subject/technique metadata tags, and a three-dimension difficulty rating; each undergoes peer review by at least one domain-expert mathematician.

**Section 2.2: Automated verification (lines 184-250).** Model submits code that computes and pickles its answer; a script verifies it. Unique integer → exact match; unique symbolic real → SymPy difference simplifies to 0; otherwise custom verification script (Figure 3 example: Pell equation x²−7y²=1 with infinitely many solutions, verifier checks the property, not a fixed value). Explicit rejection of Lean/Coq proof formats (footnote 4): models aren't trained for them and mathlib doesn't even cover the undergraduate curriculum, so proof-format benchmarks would confound reasoning with formalization skill. Admitted cost: no proof-writing problems, which "would demand human evaluation."

**Section 2.3: Validation and quality assurance (lines 251-311).** At least one blind peer review per problem checking correctness, ambiguity, guessproofness, and difficulty/metadata appropriateness. First reviews commonly catch: unverifiable answers, guessing easier than proving, brute-force shortcuts, plain errors in statements or solutions — "predominantly with authors who are new." Second blind reviews were commissioned on 30 randomly chosen questions, plus 5 randomly removed as public samples = 35 problems scrutinized beyond first review. Findings: **2/35 had incorrect answers** undetected by first review. Jeffreys-prior Bernoulli estimate: posterior error rate (2+0.5)/(35+1) ≈ 6.9%; authors settle on "approximately 10%" as a reasonable critical-error estimate, citing ImageNet >6% label error (Northcutt et al. 2021) and MMLU >9% (Gema et al. 2024) as peers. Also: **6/35 had missing hypotheses** (not fully rigorous; experts can impute, models may stumble), **2/35 had guessing strategies** violating guessproofness (fixed via revision). They concede the "passive review system" is weak and announce an active-review policy: concrete confirmation of soundness before acceptance (test the criterion directly; heuristic approximation of symbolic answers; or reviewer re-solves from key steps only). Difficulty ratings between first and second reviewers "rarely matched and often showed substantial differences."

**Section 2.4: Originality and contamination prevention (lines 312-333).** Encrypted communication channels; written materials in password-protected archives (footnote 6 admits standard email was sometimes used for a subset). Expert manual checks against math websites, repositories, publications, plus plagiarism software: Quetext and Copyscape, chosen over Turnitin specifically because they posed "the lowest risk of our problems being stored in databases that might later be used to train AI models"; only statements were checked, not solutions, to minimize exposure. No significant matches found.

**Section 2.5: Problem difficulty (lines 334-356).** Three dimensions: Background 1-5 (high school → research level); Creativity = hours for an expert to find the key ideas (unbounded); Execution = hours to compute the answer once ideas are found (unbounded). Peer-reviewed with discussion of discrepancies. Authors caveat heavily (citing Evan Chen's MOHS scale and his own "MOHS was a mistake" post): problems can collapse after exposure to a technique; unexpected shortcuts can void estimates; ratings are "rough guidance." Footnote 8: unpublished preliminary work shows harder-rated problems correlate with lower GPT-4o solve rates.

**Section 3: Dataset composition (lines 357-481).** Table 1 MSC tag distribution: Number theory 17.8%, Combinatorics 15.8%, Group theory 8.9%, Probability 5.1%, Linear algebra 4.8%, Algebraic geometry 4.8%, Special functions 4.8%, Algebraic topology 3.1%, then a long tail (each 1.0-2.4%), "Other (< 3 problems each) 9.9%". NT+combinatorics ≈ 34% of tags. By problem: number theory touches 44% of problems, combinatorics 39%, group theory 22%. Figure 4 network: NT-combinatorics co-occur in 13% of problems, combinatorics-group theory 9%, NT-group theory 8%. Over 200 distinct solution techniques; the most common (generating functions, recurrences, special functions) each appear in <5% of problems; even the most frequently co-occurring techniques appear together in at most 3 problems — deliberate methodological diversity. The exact problem count is never stated ("hundreds").

**Section 4.1: Experiment-enabled evaluation framework (lines 482-519).** Iterative agentic loop (Figure 5): prompt → model response → execute extracted Python code blocks → append stdout/stderr/timeouts → repeat until the model submits a final answer (marker comment `# This is the final answer`, pickle to `final_answer.p`, self-contained script) or hits the **10,000-token conversation limit**, after which a final prompt demands an immediate answer; improperly formatted final answers are marked incorrect.

**Section 4.2.1: Accuracy (lines 520-546).** Six models evaluated: o1-preview, o1-mini, GPT-4o (2024-08-06), Claude 3.5 Sonnet (2024-10-22), Grok 2 Beta, Gemini 1.5 Pro 002. All below 2% success on the full benchmark (Figure 6 caption: "mean accuracy across 8 runs"; body text still says "a single evaluation" — a leftover inconsistency, see limitations). Because successes are so few, "the precise ordering of model performance should be interpreted with significant caution." Four problems were solved at least once by any model; these got 5 repeated runs per model per problem — only one cell was 5/5 (o1-preview on problem 2); o1-preview strongest on repeats. Key honesty note: a correct answer does not imply correct reasoning — one of the four problems yielded to "a few simple simulations" (guessing without understanding). Transcripts for the 5 public samples (5 runs/model) are released.

**Section 4.2.2: Responses and token usage (lines 547-557).** o1-preview averages 1.29 responses/problem vs Grok 2 Beta 3.81; o1-preview and Gemini 1.5 Pro typically submit before seeing any experimental results despite instructions to experiment. Gemini hits the 10k limit on only 16.8% of questions and uses ~6,000 tokens/question; Claude 3.5 Sonnet, GPT-4o, Grok 2 exceed the limit on >45% of attempts and use 12,000-17,000 tokens (overrun possible because the final experiment's results and final answer still complete).

**Section 5: Related work (lines 558-605).** GSM8K/MATH saturated; ARB (university+contest); GHOSTS (709 graduate-level problems, human-expert evaluation); OlympiadBench (8,476 problems); PutnamBench; Omni-MATH (4,428 competition problems) — all contamination-exposed because sourced from historical materials. Putnam-AXIOM generates variants by renaming variables/changing constants: o1-preview drops 50% → 34% on variants vs originals — direct evidence of contamination inflation — but adds "only a limited degree of novelty" and can't raise difficulty. AIMO makes novel problems but requires open-weight models. MiniF2F: 488 formalized olympiad problems, contamination-exposed. FrontierMath's three claimed advances: exclusively unpublished problems; research-level difficulty; automated verification usable on closed models.

**Section 6: Interviews with mathematicians (lines 606-648).** Tao, Gowers, Borcherds, Chen (Chen is a co-author; Tao contributed problems — disclosed in footnote 11). All four: exceptionally challenging; deep domain expertise needed. Tao: near-term solvable only by "a semi-expert like a graduate student in a related field, maybe paired with some combination of a modern AI and lots of other algebra packages." Borcherds: problems "aren't quite the same as coming up with original proofs." Tao expects the benchmark to "resist AIs for several years at least"; guiding current AI on advanced graduate questions takes "about five times as much effort" as solving directly, expected to fall below 1 within a few years; human-AI collaboration to precede full automation (~3 years per Chen and Tao). Barrier: training data "almost nonexistent...you're talking like a dozen papers with relevant things" (Tao); "tricks of the trade" (Gowers). Uses: conjecture checking, "slightly boring bits of doing research" (Gowers), routine technically demanding calculations (Tao); compute cost matters ("three days of compute off of all of Google" quip about AlphaProof).

**Section 7: Discussion (lines 649-674).** Restates contributions. Admitted limitations: (1) automated verifiability excludes proof-writing and open-ended exploration; (2) hours-scale problems fall short of weeks/months-scale research, missing "crucial long-term research skills"; (3) near-zero solve rates temporarily prevent discriminating between models. Ten problems were shared with the four interviewed experts (three Fields Medalists) for qualitative validation.

**Section 8: Future work (lines 676-690).** More problems with "enhanced quality assurance procedures to reduce answer errors"; more public samples; experiment with larger token limits; "conduct multiple runs for each model-problem pair, enabling us to report statistics and confidence intervals across attempts" — i.e., CIs were NOT reported in this paper. Epoch AI commits to maintaining it as a public resource with regular evaluations.

## Benchmark anatomy

- **N items**: never stated exactly — "hundreds" of problems (abstract, Section 1). Known public subset: 5 sample problems randomly removed from the dataset. 30 further problems randomly second-reviewed (35 total with extra scrutiny). Table 1 implies a scale of roughly 150-300+ via "Other (< 3 problems each) 9.9%" of MSC tags, but the paper deliberately withholds N.
- **Construction**: hand-authored original problems by 60+ expert mathematicians (grad students to faculty; 14 IMO golds; 1 Fields Medalist contributor; Tao contributed several problems) under four requirements: originality, automated verifiability, guessproofness (<1% guess success rule of thumb), computational tractability (author scripts < 1 min).
- **Validation/review**: ≥1 blind peer review per problem by a domain expert (statement correctness, solution correctness, ambiguity, guessproofness, difficulty/metadata). Second blind review on 35 problems found 2 wrong answers, 6 missing hypotheses, 2 guessproofness violations. Estimated critical label-error rate ≈10% (Jeffreys posterior 6.9%). Passive review being replaced by active confirmation-of-soundness review.
- **Human baseline**: none measured. Difficulty is expert self-assessment (hours for background/creativity/execution) with noisy peer reconciliation ("ratings rarely matched"). Qualitative validation only: 10 problems shown to 4 eminent mathematicians (interviews, Section 6). Authors explicitly call for "systematic data collection on human solution times."
- **Contamination defenses**: unpublished problems; encrypted channels and password-protected archives (with an admitted email lapse); plagiarism tools selected to avoid training-data retention (Quetext/Copyscape, statements only); manual expert originality checks; evaluation by request only. No discussion of sponsor-side access controls despite acknowledged OpenAI support.
- **Scoring**: fully automated binary verification of a pickled Python/SymPy object — exact integer match, SymPy symbolic-difference-is-zero, or custom property-checking script for non-unique answers. No LLM judge, no human grading, no partial credit. Agentic harness with Python execution, 10,000-token limit, 20-second per-block execution cap.
- **Statistical reporting**: weak at publication. Headline figure is mean accuracy over 8 full evaluations (body text inconsistently says one evaluation); no confidence intervals anywhere; no significance tests; no clustering; repeat variance quantified only on the 4 ever-solved problems (5 runs each) and 5 public samples. CIs are promised as future work. The one rigorous statistical artifact is the Jeffreys-prior label-error estimate.

## Reported results

- All six models < 2% mean accuracy on the full benchmark (Figure 6, mean over 8 runs); no CI given. Models: o1-preview, o1-mini, GPT-4o (2024-08-06), Claude 3.5 Sonnet (2024-10-22), Grok 2 Beta, Gemini 1.5 Pro 002.
- Pass@8 (Figure 9): o1-preview ≈ 6%; Grok 2 Beta ≈ 2% — a ~3x inflation over mean accuracy for o1-preview.
- Repeat trials on the 4 ever-solved problems (5 runs per model per problem, Table 2): success rates span 0-100%; only o1-preview on problem 2 was 5/5; the same model on the same problem often fails most re-runs (e.g., Grok 60% on P1 but 0% elsewhere). Explicit caution that ordering models is unreliable "as individual successes can have an outsized impact on rankings."
- One of the four solved problems was solvable by simple simulation guessing — correct answer without correct reasoning.
- Label error: 2/35 wrong answers in double-reviewed sample → posterior ≈6.9% (Jeffreys), authors adopt ≈10% critical error rate; plus 6/35 missing hypotheses and 2/35 guessproofness violations.
- Behavioral: o1-preview 1.29 responses/problem vs Grok 3.81; Gemini uses ~6k tokens (16.8% hit the 10k limit); Claude/GPT-4o/Grok exceed the limit on >45% of attempts, 12k-17k tokens.
- Saturation contrast (B.3): best-model accuracy 98.1% MMLU-CollegeMath, 96.4% GSM8K, 94.8% MATH, 74% AIME, 73.9% MathVista, 60.54% Omni-MATH, <2% FrontierMath.
- Putnam-AXIOM contamination evidence cited: o1-preview 50% → 34% on functional variants of Putnam problems.

## Limitations

**Admitted by the authors:**
1. Automated verification excludes proof-writing and open-ended exploration — a large part of real research (Sections 2.2, 7); Borcherds: not "the same as coming up with original proofs."
2. Hours-scale problems miss weeks-to-months "long-term research skills" (Section 7).
3. Near-zero scores temporarily prevent discriminating among models (Section 7); ordering unreliable (Section 4.2.1).
4. ~10% estimated critical label-error rate; passive review missed wrong answers, missing hypotheses, guessable problems (Section 2.3).
5. Difficulty ratings are subjective and inconsistent between raters; only "rough guidance"; validation of ratings is preliminary/unpublished (Sections 2.5, C).
6. Contamination protocol lapses: standard email used for a subset of problems (footnote 6); plagiarism checks covered statements only (footnote 7).
7. No CIs/statistics at publication; multiple runs and CIs deferred to future work (Section 8).
8. Numerical-answer format "somewhat contrived" per interviewees (Sections 1, 6).

**Observed, not admitted:**
1. The exact benchmark size N is never disclosed, making the "<2%" numerator/denominator unauditable and CIs uncomputable by readers.
2. Internal inconsistency between "a single evaluation" (Section 4.2.1 text) and "mean accuracy across 8 runs" (Figure 6 caption, B.4) — an artifact of v7 revisions that is never reconciled.
3. OpenAI's support is acknowledged, but the paper says nothing about what access the sponsor had to problems/solutions — a contamination vector outside the threat model that later became publicly controversial.
4. No human control group ever attempts the problems under test conditions; "hours for experts" is authorial self-report, so the human-AI gap claim rests on interviews about a 10-problem sample.
5. The 10,000-token limit (set before long-thinking models matured) is a harness confound: models that spend tokens on experiments are penalized, and 45%+ limit-hit rates for three models mean measured accuracy partly reflects budget, not ability.
6. The four-ever-solved-problems analysis conditions on success (selection on the dependent variable); repeat statistics for never-solved problems (false-negative rate of the harness, e.g., formatting failures) are not reported.
7. Two of the five released sample problems (4 and 5) needed post-publication corrections by outside readers (footnote 12) — corroborating that even showcased items carry statement/solution defects.
8. Guessproofness is a design rule of thumb, never empirically audited across the dataset (and one evaluated problem demonstrably failed it).

## Implications for CRUCIBLE-CHAIN

1. **Never ship answer menus; enforce a quantitative guessproofness budget per instance.** FrontierMath's <1% guess-probability rule plus large non-obvious numerical answers is exactly the property CRUCIBLE-CHAIN lost when prompts leaked method recipes and answer menus (saturation at 94-100%). Action: strip enumerable option sets from all prompts; make the generator emit final answers from a space of ≥10⁴ plausible values; empirically audit guessability by running a no-reasoning baseline (model forced to answer immediately) and require its success rate < 1% per template and per stage.

2. **Audit statement error even with a deterministic generator, and report a Jeffreys-style error bound.** FrontierMath's expert pipeline still had 2/35 wrong answers and 6/35 missing hypotheses; their fix is "active review" requiring positive confirmation of soundness. CRUCIBLE-CHAIN's labels are near-zero-error by construction, but the failure mode migrates to prompt/statement defects (ambiguity, missing hypotheses, leaked hints — the exact cause of the saturation incident). Action: second-review ≥30 randomly sampled rendered instances per release by someone who did not write the template, classify defects (wrong-by-construction / ambiguous / leaky), and publish the (k+0.5)/(n+1) posterior defect rate. At single-digit target pass rates, statement-defect rate must be held well below ~1-2% or it dominates the measured signal.

3. **Scale answer for the question of record: independent templates are the unit of inference, and current 8 is far too few.** FrontierMath's core statistical lesson: at <2% accuracy with one (or even 8) evaluations, "individual successes can have an outsized impact on rankings," and they could not produce CIs. For CRUCIBLE-CHAIN targeting p ≈ 0.05: a naive binomial 95% CI of half-width ±0.02 needs ~450 independent items; but instances within a template share failure modes, so the effective sample size is closer to the number of templates — with 8 templates, a cluster-robust CI on a 5% pass rate is roughly ±5-8% (useless). Action: grow to ≥30-50 independent templates (each with a distinct judgment-call structure whose recipe does not transfer), keep ~15-30 instances each (total 500-1000+ runs per condition), compute CIs by bootstrap over templates (clusters), and run ≥5 repeats per model x instance because FrontierMath observed 0-100% swings across 5 runs on identical items. Report mean pass rate with cluster-bootstrap CI, never a single run.

4. **Report the mean-vs-pass@k gap as a headline diagnostic.** o1-preview: <2% mean but ~6% pass@8. For a benchmark with attractive wrong paths, pass@k inflation measures how often the "attractor" is escapable by resampling luck rather than judgment. Action: publish mean pass@1 (over k runs), pass@k, and per-template variance; treat a large gap as evidence a template is guessable/unstable rather than as model capability.

5. **Exploit the generator for contamination defenses FrontierMath could only approximate.** Their defenses (encryption, tool selection to avoid training-data retention, unpublished items, evaluation-by-request) were operational and admitted lapses (plain email; sponsor access unaddressed). CRUCIBLE-CHAIN can do strictly better: keep the generator and seeds private, regenerate fresh instance sets per evaluation round (new surface parameters, same measured construct), never reuse released instances in scoring, and log/disclose exactly who has seen instances (the sponsor-access lesson from the OpenAI acknowledgment). Publish only retired instances plus template descriptions, mirroring their 5-sample release pattern.

6. **Red-team every template for shortcut circumvention before acceptance.** FrontierMath reviewers repeatedly caught "cases where simple brute-force methods circumvent the intended difficulty," and one of their four solved problems fell to "a few simple simulations." For 5-8 stage chains, a shortcut that bypasses the judgment calls (e.g., simulating the pipeline end-to-end, or exploiting non-compensatory scoring by copying a clean-condition answer) breaks the construct. Action: adversarial pass per template with a frontier model explicitly prompted to find shortcuts/brute force/menu elimination; a template is accepted only if shortcut attacks stay under the guessproofness budget; re-run this audit whenever prompts change.

7. **Replace subjective difficulty ratings with measured per-stage failure curves.** FrontierMath's author-hours ratings "rarely matched" between reviewers and were validated only informally against GPT-4o solve rates. CRUCIBLE-CHAIN's generator controls difficulty parametrically (chain length, distractor attractiveness, defect subtlety), so difficulty should be calibrated empirically: measure per-stage failure rates and full-chain pass rates across models, then tune parameters toward the target single-digit frontier pass rate — the knob FrontierMath wished it had ("room to improve the difficulty of the remaining questions").

8. **Keep verification fully mechanical, but instrument reasoning validity separately.** FrontierMath's binary auto-verification is cheap, unbiased, and works on closed models — but they concede a right answer can mask wrong reasoning. CRUCIBLE-CHAIN's non-compensatory stage scoring already forces the full path; preserve that, and for the flawed-premise condition, score refusal/flag as a distinct verified outcome (their 6/35 missing-hypothesis problems show models face malformed statements even in elite benchmarks, but FrontierMath has no refusal-correct condition — CRUCIBLE-CHAIN's is a genuine novelty worth defending). Also fix harness confounds they hit: token limits that 45% of attempts exceeded, and format-failure incorrectness — report format-failure rates separately from substantive failures.
