## Section-by-section notes

The paper has 7 numbered sections plus references. There are NO appendices, no supplementary tables beyond Tables 1-3, and no experiments of the authors' own.

**Abstract + Sec 1 Introduction (lines 1-84).** Defines Benchmark Data Contamination (BDC): language models incorporate evaluation-benchmark information from training data, producing inaccurate/unreliable evaluation. Notes a minority position that contamination is beneficial (Blevins & Zettlemoyer [12]) or not a problem (Cao et al. [16]), against a majority view that it undermines trust. Announces the survey's two-part organization: Detection Techniques (matching-based vs comparison-based) and Mitigation Strategies (curating new data, refactoring existing data, benchmark-free evaluation). Claims to be the first comprehensive, systematic survey defining the BDC problem.

**Sec 2.1 Large Language Models (lines 91-164).** Standard background: encoder-only (BERT), encoder-decoder (T5), decoder-only dominance, Mamba as alternative; KM scaling law [74] vs Chinchilla scaling law [56]; four characterizing hyper-parameters (pre-training cost, model size, dataset size, post-training performance). Three basic abilities (language generation, knowledge utilization, complex reasoning), advanced abilities (tool use, external environments: autonomous driving, game agents, search engines, image models, compilers), emergent abilities (in-context learning, instruction following, step-by-step/CoT reasoning). Known problems: hallucination, weak specialized domains (catastrophic forgetting, data scarcity), difficulty of knowledge updating. No numbers of note.

**Sec 2.2 Benchmark Data Contamination (lines 165-201).** Formal Definition 1: "Exposure of a large language model to benchmark data during the training process leads to distorted evaluation results." Four severity levels: (1) Semantic Level - identical/derivative content of the benchmark (same topic or source); (2) Information Level - benchmark-related information (metadata, time distributions, label distributions, external reviews) creates tendencies/biases; (3) Data Level - benchmark data without labels; (4) Label Level - complete exposure including labels, enabling direct memorization. Severity increases from semantic to label; ease of detection runs the other way (label-level easiest to detect/prevent; semantic/information levels hardest).

**Sec 2.3 Sources and Impact (lines 202-271, Table 1 at lines 194-199).** BDC arises mainly from diverse large-scale pre-training corpora (news, forums, social media); fine-tuning is lower-risk because targeted and controlled. Three evaluation method families: traditional benchmark testing, automatic evaluation, human evaluation. Table 1 metrics: Accuracy (exact match, quasi-exact match, F1, ROUGE), Calibration (expected calibration error, AUC), Fairness (demographic parity difference, equalized odds difference), Robustness (attack success rate, performance drop rate). BDC inflates accuracy, skews calibration, can reinforce bias (fairness), and invalidates robustness testing. Automatic evaluation is called the most promising strategy but LLM judges may themselves be contaminated. Human evaluation follows the 3H rule (Helpfulness, Honesty, Harmlessness) and criteria like accuracy, relevance, fluency, transparency, safety, human alignment; Chatbot Arena cited as gathering numerous human votes; human judges can also be biased by prior exposure to contaminated data.

**Sec 2.4 Related Tasks (lines 272-331).** Seven task areas with contamination scenarios: Code Generation (tutorials/solutions on forums are semantic-level contamination that filtering misses), Machine Translation (multi-language news creates natural contamination and topic bias), Question Answering (GitHub Issues discussions inflate scores), Sentiment Analysis (topic background, e.g. COVID, pre-determines a mostly-negative label distribution), Named Entity Recognition (entity background knowledge distorts evaluation), Fake News Detection (multi-outlet coverage creates semantic-to-label-level contamination), Text Reconstruction (original full texts, e.g. antiquarian books, present in pre-training corrupt restoration evaluation).

**Sec 3 BDC Detection Techniques (lines 333-656, Table 2 at lines 334-384).** Taxonomy: Matching-based (Dataset Inspection; Membership Inference; Example Generation) and Comparison-based (Content Comparison; Sequential Analysis; Chronological Analysis). Works fitting both are assigned to the category "deemed more comprehensive or preferred by the authors."

*Sec 3.1 Matching-based (lines 394-527).* Seven representative works:
- N-gram overlap as the predominant decontamination technique: GPT-3 [15] used 13-gram overlap as the contamination signal; GPT-4 [107] used a 50-character overlap. Simple and cheap but higher false-negative rate for subtle text differences.
- Li & Flanigan [85]: task contamination; LLMs perform better on datasets released before their training-data creation date; four methods (training data inspection, task example extraction, membership inference, chronological analysis); for classification tasks without task contamination, LLMs show no significant improvement over simple majority baselines.
- Ranaldi et al. [118]: GPT-3.5 on Spider vs novel Termite dataset; adversarial table disconnection (ATD) removes structural info to gauge contamination effects on Text-to-SQL.
- Deng et al. [30] (Figure 1): retrieval system over The Pile/C4 vs benchmarks (MMLU, TruthfulQA, OpenBookQA, PIQA) plus TS-Guessing (mask a wrong option in an MCQ, ask model to fill it); commercial LLMs (ChatGPT, GPT-4) guess missing options with high accuracy.
- Golchin & Surdeanu [46]: Data Contamination Quiz (DCQ) - identify the original among three word-level perturbed versions; on GPT-3.5/4 shows higher contamination than other methods and bypasses copyright safety filters.
- Golchin & Surdeanu [47]: guided instruction prompts + overlap score + classifier; 92%-100% accuracy across seven datasets; found contamination in AG News, WNLI, XSum with GPT-4.
- Li et al. [91]: contamination report across 15+ LLMs and six multiple-choice QA benchmarks; contamination 1%-45%; contamination does not always improve performance; larger models benefit more from contaminated test sets; open-source pipeline released.
- Chang et al. [20]: name cloze membership inference on books; memorization correlates with passage frequency on the web; models do better on memorized books in downstream tasks, corrupting cultural analytics.
Critiques of matching-based detection: Yang et al. [162], Ippolito et al. [62], Jiang et al. [69] show paraphrasing bypasses n-gram decontamination; a 13B model fine-tuned on rephrased test data reaches GPT-4-comparable benchmark scores. Dekoninck et al. [29]: Evasive Augmentation Learning (EAL) - rephrasing benchmark samples during fine-tuning evades all current detection methods while improving benchmark performance up to 15%.

*Sec 3.2 Comparison-based (lines 528-656).* Six representative works:
- Magar & Schwartz [96]: pre-train BERT on Wikipedia + labeled downstream data, compare "seen" vs "unseen" performance; distinguishes memorization from exploitation; effects grow with duplication count and model size.
- Dong et al. [34]: CDD (Contamination Detection via output Distribution) and TED (Trustworthy Evaluation via output Distribution); new benchmarks DetCon and ComiEval; ChatGPT shown susceptible to BDC; CDD significantly improves detection over existing methods.
- Li [89]: perplexity-based contamination estimation without training-set access; reading comprehension and summarization benchmarks show contamination; multiple-choice benchmarks less affected.
- Oren et al. [108]: statistical test comparing likelihood of canonical benchmark ordering vs shuffled orderings; provides provable guarantees; audit of popular models found minimal evidence of widespread contamination.
- Huang et al. [61]: GPT-4 on Codeforces competition problems; significant performance decline on problems released after September 2021 (its data cutoff); fine-tuning and CoT did not consistently help.
- Roberts et al. [121]: Codeforces and Project Euler; LLM pass rates correlate with GitHub popularity and benchmark release dates; open-source dataset and framework released.
Section closes weighing the two families: matching-based is intuitive but needs training-data access (impossible for GPT-4/Claude-3) and compute; comparison-based is flexible but can have narrow detection scope (e.g., chronological analysis needs timestamped data).

**Sec 4 BDC Mitigation Strategies (lines 657-982, Table 3 at lines 582-623).** Taxonomy: Data Curation (Private Benchmark; Dynamic Benchmark), Data Refactoring (Data Regeneration; Content Filtering), Benchmark-free (LLM-as-judge; Human Participation).

*Sec 4.1 Curating New Data (lines 662-752).* New data is uncontaminated only until it enters a future pre-training corpus. Chandran et al. [19]: private test sets with confidential computing/cryptography (Figure 2); requires trust in model provider and benchmark custodian. Jacovi et al. [65]: three practical strategies - encrypt test data with a public key, demand training-exclusion controls from API holders, avoid data posted with its solution online. Ma et al. [95]: Dynaboard - evaluation-as-a-service in the cloud; Dynascore utility aggregation over accuracy, memory, robustness, customizable to user preferences. Li et al. [90]: LatestEval - reading-comprehension tests built from texts published in a recent window; minimal memorization behaviors observed vs older benchmarks. Jain et al. [66]: LiveCodeBench - continuously updated competitive-programming problems; reveals overfitting in existing code benchmarks. Fan et al. [41]: NPHardEval - dynamic reasoning benchmark with monthly updates. Tradeoffs: private benchmarks are opaque and demand ethics/trust; dynamic benchmarks "introduce bias and lack the guarantee of consistent results across successive assessments"; both are resource-heavy.

*Sec 4.2 Refactoring Existing Data (lines 754-864).* Xia et al. [158]: EvoEval (Figure 3) - five targeted transformation dimensions (Difficult, Creative, Subtle, Combine, Tool use) applied to existing coding problems, checking answer consistency plus code pass rate; across 51 LLMs, an average 39.4% performance reduction on HumanEval. DyVal 2 / Meta Probing Agents (Zhu et al. [181], Figure 4): psychometrics-grounded probing agents with five principles (paraphrase questions, paraphrase choices, permute choices, add extra context, add a new choice) over three cognitive abilities (language understanding, problem solving, domain knowledge); most LLMs have room for improvement; strong correlation between basic abilities and model size; doubles as data augmentation. Ying et al. [164] (Figure 5): mimicking strategy (LLM-generated stylistically similar samples) + extending strategy (difficulty adjustment by cognitive level); mitigates leakage and gives more nuanced evaluation. Yang et al. [162]: LLM-based decontamination; advocates fresh one-time exams. Dodge et al. [32]: C4 audit - unexpected sources (patents, US military websites), machine-generated text, and evaluation examples from other NLP datasets found inside C4; blocklist filtering disproportionately removes text related to minority individuals.

*Sec 4.3 Benchmark-free Evaluation (lines 865-982).* Li et al. [87]: TreeEval (Figure 6) - a high-performance LLM examiner runs an irreproducible evaluation session via tree planning; on six models, achieved high correlation with AlpacaEval2.0 using approximately 45 questions. Chiang et al. [24]: Chatbot Arena - crowdsourced pairwise human preference; over 240K votes collected. Yu et al. [166]: FreeEval (Figure 7) - unified framework integrating dataset-based evaluation, data regeneration, LLM-as-judge, human evaluation, meta-evaluation (contamination detection, bias evaluation), with distributed inference backends. Caveats: judge LLMs may themselves be trained on contaminated data; human evaluation is subjective, resource-intensive, time-consuming. Closing synthesis: no strategy is immune to secondary contamination (new/refactored data may be produced by contaminated LLMs); semantic-level contamination remains unavoidable; even LLM evaluators draw on contaminated training knowledge.

**Sec 5 Challenges and Future Directions (lines 983-1048).** Full BDC removal is deemed impracticable for two reasons: (1) Imperative of Large-Scale Pre-training - the required data volume inevitably includes benchmark-relevant content; filtering/regeneration cannot address semantic and informational dimensions, and techniques that could would demand prohibitive compute; (2) Ascendancy of AIGC - AI-generated content recursively spreads contamination toward the semantic dimension, making human identification harder. Five future directions: Human Evaluation (ideal but resource-intensive and biased by evaluator background: politics, culture, beliefs, education); Dynamic Systems (adaptive evaluation a la TreeEval, but the underlying data remains static and possibly AIGC, so residual risk persists); Benchmark Content Tags (a robots.txt / Google Fact Check Tools API analog: standardized tags marking benchmark-related content so pre-training filters can catch it); Adversarial Evaluation (RL, GANs, VAEs synthesizing new evaluation data with an integrated BDC detector supervising generated data); Comprehensive Evaluation Systems (integrate multiple mitigation options, e.g., FreeEval, to minimize risk).

**Sec 6 Conclusion (lines 1049-1071).** Restates the taxonomy (detection: matching vs comparison; mitigation: curation, refactoring, benchmark-free), that none are immune to secondary or semantic-level contamination, and the five future directions. Calls for more work.

**Sec 7 Author Contributions (lines 1072-1077).** CX led conception and most sections; SG contributed Section 2; DG and MTK supervised.

**References (lines 1078-1725).** 184 numbered references, [1] Aggarwal 2018 through [184] Zhu et al. 2023. Notable duplicates: [109]/[110] are the same Ouyang et al. InstructGPT paper; [154]/[155] are the same Wei et al. CoT paper. [50] (Gulwani et al.) is missing its title. No appendix follows the references.

## Limitations

**Admitted by the authors:**
- Full elimination of BDC risk is "deemed impracticable" (Sec 5): large-scale pre-training necessarily ingests benchmark-adjacent content, and AIGC recursively spreads semantic-level contamination.
- Every mitigation family has stated tradeoffs: private benchmarks are opaque and demand trust/ethics; dynamic benchmarks introduce bias and lack consistency across successive assessments; regeneration needs heavy compute/expertise; content filtering misses nuance; LLM judges may themselves be contaminated; human evaluation is subjective, resource-intensive, and evaluators can be biased by prior exposure.
- Secondary contamination: newly collected or refactored data may be generated by already-contaminated LLMs (Sec 4.3 synthesis).
- Semantic-level contamination "remains unavoidable, and simple content filtering may not suffice" (Sec 4.3).
- Matching-based detection needs training-data access (unavailable for proprietary models) and heavy compute; string matching is defeated by paraphrase (Sec 3.1).
- Category assignment of surveyed works is partly subjective (authors' preference, Sec 3).

**Observed, not admitted:**
- No systematic review methodology: no search strategy, inclusion criteria, or screening counts; "representative" works are hand-picked, so coverage claims are unauditable.
- Zero uncertainty propagation: every relayed number is a point estimate; the survey never notes that the primary works themselves rarely report CIs, and offers no meta-analytic comparison of detector accuracy on a common testbed.
- The four-level severity taxonomy is not operationalized: no criteria, thresholds, or examples-to-level assignment protocol; nothing in the survey measures which level a given real contamination case belongs to, and no inter-rater process exists.
- Definition 1 bakes the consequence into the definition ("leads to distorted evaluation results"), making contamination-without-distortion (which Li et al. [91] and Magar & Schwartz [96], both surveyed here, actually document) definitionally paradoxical; the tension is never reconciled.
- Contamination is framed exclusively as a training-time phenomenon; evaluation-time leakage (prompt-carried answer menus, few-shot exemplars revealing method recipes, retrieval augmentation pulling in test data) is entirely out of scope even though the Information Level concept fits it naturally.
- v1 is dated June 2024 and coverage effectively stops in early 2024; the fast-moving detection/mitigation literature after that is absent.
- Editorial defects suggest a light review pass: duplicated references ([109]=[110], [154]=[155]), a reference missing its title ([50]), a garbled duplicated sentence opening Sec 3.2, "We now consider six representative comparison-based studies" appearing AFTER those studies were discussed, and ACM DOI placeholders.
- No decision guidance: a practitioner is given taxonomies but no ranking of which detector/mitigation to use under given constraints (access, compute, task).
- The "benchmark content tags" proposal ignores adversarial incentives (nothing compels model trainers to honor tags, exactly as robots.txt is routinely ignored for training crawls).

## Implications for CRUCIBLE-CHAIN

1. **Name and audit the saturation failure as Information-Level contamination, evaluation-time variant.** The survey's four-level taxonomy (Sec 2.2) classifies "metadata, ... label distributions, and external reviews" as Information-Level contamination; CRUCIBLE-CHAIN's leak of method recipes and answer menus through its own prompts is the same mechanism delivered in-context instead of in pre-training. Action: add a pre-release template gate that classifies every prompt component against the four levels - anything that exposes the method recipe (information level) or enumerates candidate answers (label level in-context) must be cut or moved server-side. This turns the vague "prompts leaked" diagnosis into a checkable rubric.

2. **Run TS-Guessing/DCQ-style probes against your own templates as a leakage unit test.** Deng et al.'s TS-Guessing (mask part of the test item, see if the model reconstructs it) and Golchin & Surdeanu's DCQ (pick the original among perturbed variants) are detection tools, but for a constructed-truth benchmark they invert into QA gates: mask the planted defect location, the correct stage decision, or the refusal trigger, and measure whether frontier models guess it from surface features alone at above-chance rates without executing the chain. Any template where masked-slot guessing succeeds is leaking structure, not measuring judgment. Budget: cheap (a few hundred calls per template) and it directly predicts saturation before a full campaign.

3. **Assume string-level novelty buys nothing; design for paraphrase-robustness and a private generator frontier.** The survey's sharpest empirical point is that n-gram/string decontamination is defeated by rephrasing - a 13B fine-tuned on paraphrased test data matches GPT-4, and EAL gains up to 15% while evading every tested detector (Sec 3.1). For CRUCIBLE-CHAIN this means: never publish the full instance set or the generator's parameter space; keep a sealed seed range for official runs; regenerate fresh instances per evaluation campaign (the survey's dynamic-benchmark strategy, LiveCodeBench/NPHardEval-style); and treat any published example instances as burned the moment they ship, since rephrase-tuning on them transfers to siblings from the same template.

4. **The scale question must be answered at the template (cluster) level, not the instance level - and 8 clusters is far too few.** The survey documents that contamination/leakage effects are heterogeneous across models and benchmarks (1-45% contamination, gains concentrated in larger models, per Li et al. [91]) and that a single structural flaw (e.g., a leaked recipe) invalidates every instance sharing that structure. Since CRUCIBLE-CHAIN instances within a template are near-duplicates by construction (deterministic generator), the effective sample size for between-model inference is the number of independent templates, not 8 x 18 = 144 items. Action: power the benchmark with cluster-robust standard errors treating template as the cluster; to detect, say, a 10-15 point model difference with single-digit-to-30% pass rates, plan on the order of 30-60 independent templates (clusters) with balanced instances and >=3 repeated decodes per instance to separate decode variance from item variance - and report CIs, which literally no work surveyed here does; that alone differentiates CRUCIBLE-CHAIN's statistical reporting from the entire field this survey covers.

5. **Use EvoEval-style targeted transformation to re-harden the 8 saturated templates instead of only authoring new ones.** EvoEval's five dimensions (Difficult, Creative, Subtle, Combine, Tool use) recovered 39.4% average headroom on 51 LLMs from a saturated benchmark (HumanEval); MPA's five probing principles (paraphrase question/choices, permute choices, add context, add a distractor choice) did the same for MCQA. The CRUCIBLE-CHAIN analog: keep each template's generator core, but (a) remove the answer menu entirely (free-form final answers, exact-match scored - the survey notes multiple-choice formats are both more guessable and more contamination-sensitive per Li [89] and TS-Guessing), (b) compose two templates' judgment chains into one instance (Combine), and (c) inject correct-looking but irrelevant context (Subtle). Measure per-transformation pass-rate deltas to learn which leak carried the saturation.

6. **Version and timestamp the generator, then use chronological analysis on yourselves.** Huang et al. and Roberts et al. detect contamination through performance discontinuities at training-cutoff/release dates. CRUCIBLE-CHAIN should log generator version, release date, and instance-seed provenance for every scored run, so that a future model scoring anomalously well on pre-release-vintage instances vs freshly generated ones produces exactly this discontinuity signal. That requires maintaining at least two vintages per campaign (one released earlier, one generated at scoring time) - a cheap, permanent leakage alarm.

7. **Adopt the private-benchmark hygiene checklist from Jacovi et al. and Chandran et al. for the release package.** Concretely: distribute any public instance archive encrypted with a public key (so crawlers ingest ciphertext), never post instances adjacent to their answers, demand training-exclusion (canary strings embedded in every released instance so ingestion is later detectable via membership inference), and keep official scoring behind an API/eval-harness rather than shipping the answer-computing generator. The survey's warning that "new data is only uncontaminated until it is incorporated into a pre-trained corpus of a future LLM" is a deadline, not a hypothetical: CRUCIBLE-CHAIN 1.0's shipped prompts should be presumed present in 2026-2027 training corpora.

8. **Keep scoring deterministic; do not add an LLM judge for partial credit.** The survey repeatedly flags that LLM-as-judge reintroduces contamination through the judge's own training data (Secs 2.3, 4.3, 5) and that even human judges are biased by prior exposure. CRUCIBLE-CHAIN's constructed-truth, non-compensatory, exact-match scoring is precisely the design that sidesteps the judge-contamination surface; pressure to soften saturated-to-single-digit scores with judged partial credit would surrender that advantage. If stage-level diagnostics are wanted, score stages programmatically against the generator's intermediate values instead.

9. **Report what the field does not.** Since no surveyed work reports uncertainty, CRUCIBLE-CHAIN's question of record ("what scale for statistically significant claims") is itself a differentiator: pre-register the inference plan (cluster = template, repeats per instance, paired-permutation tests between models across the three conditions), publish CIs on every headline number, and publish the leakage-probe (implication 2) and vintage-discontinuity (implication 6) results alongside scores. That makes the benchmark's validity case auditable in exactly the dimensions this survey shows the field is weakest.
