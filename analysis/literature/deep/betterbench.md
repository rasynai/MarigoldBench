## Section-by-section notes

**Abstract.** Framework of 46 best practices across the AI benchmark lifecycle; 24 AI benchmarks evaluated. Findings: large quality differences; most benchmarks do not report statistical significance nor allow easy replication. Deliverables: a minimum-quality-assurance checklist and a living repository at betterbench.stanford.edu.

**Sec. 1 Introduction.** Defines a benchmark (following Raji et al. [67]) as a combination of dataset(s) plus a metric, conceptualized as representing tasks/abilities, adopted by a community as a shared comparison framework. High-quality benchmark = interpretable, clear about intended purpose and scope, usable. Claims no structured quality assessment for AI benchmarks (FM and non-FM) existed before. Five contributions: 46-criteria framework from expert interviews + literature; scores for 16 FM and 8 non-FM benchmarks; insights into prevalent issues; a checklist; a living repository. Notes benchmarks feed policy (UK AISI Inspect, EU AI Act Article 51).

**Sec. 2 Related Work.** 2.1: prior critiques — LLM benchmarks insufficient [59]; validity concerns [54, 77, 67]; saturation threatens utility [62]; narrow scope / socio-technical gap [84, 49]; overgeneralization of scores [67]; data cards [65]; data-curation checklist [12]; FAIR principles [88] — all judged insufficient because benchmarks add infrastructure and evaluation methods on top of data. 2.2: best practices imported from transistor hardware [18], environmental quality [16], bioinformatics [7], metabolomics [24], protein-fold benchmarking [78], optimization [10]: (i) design for downstream utility; (ii) ensure validity — including "choosing a large test set to promote the statistical significance of results" and updating benchmarks over time to prevent overfitting, plus "the methods or approaches being evaluated should not be used to create the gold standard dataset" [7]; (iii) prioritize score interpretability (misleading benchmarks are the worst case; do not present results as absolutes [16]); (iv) guarantee accessibility (data + scripts needed for reproducibility).

**Sec. 3 Methodology.** 46 criteria from literature review plus unstructured interviews with 20+ people across five stakeholder groups (policymakers, model developers, benchmark developers, model users, AI researchers). Six steps: (1) map the space, define five-stage lifecycle; (2) translate objectives into criteria, split into (a) developer-controlled with normative consensus (the 46 assessed), (b) developer-controlled but context-dependent/hard to verify (Sec. 5), (c) outside developer control / open research (Sec. 8); (3) validate — three authors independently scored the same benchmark to calibrate, guidelines clarified, framework shared with all stakeholder groups, and BetterBench self-assessed against its own criteria (App. I.2); (4) structure — 16 FM + 8 non-FM benchmarks, prioritized by common use (reported by GPT-4/Claude-3 model cards), manual evaluation only, at least two authors independently review each benchmark, consensus required, third reviewer available for deadlocks ("this case did not occur"); only developer-released sources (websites, papers, GitHub) considered; (5) scoring — discrete 0/5/10/15 per criterion (0 = neither referenced nor satisfied, 5 = mentioned without fulfilling, 10 = partially met, 15 = fully met), stage averages, aggregate usability score = weighted average of implementation, documentation, maintenance; mean >= 10 deemed "reasonably good"; (6) living website with community feedback channel; CC BY 4.0.

**Sec. 4 Assessment Criteria.** Four assessed lifecycle stages (retirement excluded because active benchmarks cannot be assessed for it). Fig. 2 lists 14 design criteria (define tested capability; construct-to-task translation; domain experts; domain literature; use cases/personas; differences to related benchmarks; input sensitivity; validated automatic evaluation; score interpretation guidance; real-world usefulness; informed metric choice; metric floors/ceilings; human performance level; random performance level). Fig. 3 lists 11 implementation criteria (evaluation code; data or generation mechanism accessible; API eval; local eval; GUID/encryption of instances; training_on_test_set task; replication script; statistical significance/uncertainty; harm-content warnings; build status; release requirements). Fig. 4 lists 19 documentation criteria (requirements file; quick-start; in-line comments; code docs; peer-reviewed paper; design-process docs; test tasks & rationale; normative assumptions; limitations; test-environment/prompt design process; persistent identifier; standardized metadata; data sources/collection; preprocessing; annotation; evaluation metric; license; representativeness; standardized data documentation). Fig. 5 lists 3 maintenance criteria (code usability checked within last year; maintained feedback channel; contact person). Note: figure counts sum to 47 while the paper claims 46; App. J resolves to 46 by placing statistical significance under documentation (J.3 #17) — see observed limitations.

**Sec. 5 Other Design Considerations** (excluded from scoring as context-dependent): general vs. specific benchmarks; detecting small improvements ("Benchmarks should be designed so that a 1% improvement can be reliably detected [34]", dependent on evaluation data size and task diversity); multi-modal assessment; versioning (minor updates via task versioning, major via new versions, e.g., AgentBench v0.1/v0.2); dynamic vs. static benchmarks (dynamic mitigates saturation/contamination but reduces comparability); gameability (ideal benchmark resists score-boosting without capability gains); positionality statements.

**Sec. 6 Quantitative Results.** Implementation is the weakest stage for both FM and non-FM, followed by maintenance. Criterion-level lows: replicate-results script mean 3.75/15; statistical significance mean 5.62/15; standardized metadata fulfilled by almost none. Overall design mean 10.7 (Table 1 prints 10.8 for All), weighted usability mean 8.7. Table 1 stage means (FM / non-FM / All): Design 10.6 / 11.2 / 10.8; Implementation 5.5 / 7.5 / 6.2; Documentation 10.3 / 9.9 / 10.1; Maintenance 9.1 / 10.6 / 9.6. FM vs non-FM difference in mean design and usability scores not statistically significant at the 95% level (bootstrap, Fig. 8 / App. D). Design-usability Pearson correlations (Table 2): FM rho = 0.730, p = 0.001; non-FM rho = 0.477, p = 0.279 (n.s.); All rho = 0.693, p < 0.001 — poorly designed benchmarks tend also to be less usable.

**Sec. 7 Discussion.** (i) Quality varies widely: MMLU lowest weighted average 5.5 vs GPQA 11.0, yet GPT-4/Claude-3/Gemini reports and UK AISI Inspect include both without noting quality differences. (ii) Signal vs noise: developers should re-run evaluations with different seeds/temperatures and report intra-model mean and variance; inter-model differences are only interpretable against intra-model variance; 14/24 benchmarks did neither multiple evaluations nor significance/uncertainty reporting. (iii) Reproducibility: 17/24 provide no easy-to-run replication script, 4/24 replicate only part of results. (iv) Small fixes yield big gains (contact person, code docs). (v) Higher standards needed: build status implemented by only 3/24; GUID/encryption pioneered only by a few (GPQA [68], BIG-bench [75]).

**Sec. 8 Open Challenges** (outside developer control): quick saturation (some benchmarks saturate within months of release [58]); contamination (canary use cannot be enforced on model developers; shared responsibility; demonstrated in FM [29, 37, 47] and non-FM [43, 41]); poor construct validity (properties emerge from model-user interaction [61]; ecological validity hard without access to real user interactions; formal verification [23] untested); no standardization of benchmark reporting (benchmarks give relative not absolute signal; avenues: independent publication entities, market incentives, mandatory reporting in legislation).

**Sec. 9 Limitations.** Equal weighting of criteria despite differing effort/importance; only four score levels, so "a benchmark barely fulfilling a criterion and one almost entirely fulfilling it would receive the same 10-point score"; assessment gameable by cherry-picking easy criteria (authors argue even that improves quality); construct validity of assessed benchmarks not evaluated (needs domain experts, out of scope); framework intended for public benchmarks only.

**Sec. 10 Impact Statement.** Aims to raise benchmark quality and transparency; warns the assessment "only provides minimum quality assurances and is not sufficient to assess the suitability of a benchmark for a concrete use case"; notes equity risk that criteria like domain-expert involvement favor well-resourced groups.

**References.** 95 entries, spanning AI benchmarks assessed, benchmarking-practice literature in other sciences (bioinformatics, environmental science, transistors, metabolomics, optimization, education), contamination studies, and statistics-in-eval work (Agarwal et al. statistical precipice [4]; Biderman et al. lessons from the trenches [13]; Jordan calibrated chaos [40]).

**NeurIPS Checklist (meta-section).** Claims supported in Sec. 6/App. E; limitations in Sec. 9-10; code to replicate promised "upon publication" (not in paper); error bars: "We report statistical significance results for our results, where applicable"; compute: standard laptops; interviews framed as information-gathering, no IRB, participants voluntary and uncompensated; assessment released CC BY 4.0.

**App. A Stakeholders.** Five personas with objectives and 2-3 concrete use cases each: benchmark developers (checklist pre-deployment; self-scoring; comparison), model developers (choose what to report; cite quality differences), model users (interpret reported results; choose between related benchmarks), AI researchers (study benchmarking practice; landscape overview), regulators/standard-setters (design regulatory requirements; mandate minimum scores — e.g., accept only benchmarks meeting certain checklist criteria as compliance proof).

**App. B Benchmark Lifecycle.** Five stages elaborated: design (purpose, scope, tasks, datasets, metrics; stakeholder input), implementation (collect/process/annotate data, evaluation scripts, interfaces/APIs, initial validation), documentation (task/dataset/metric descriptions, design decisions, limitations, quick-start resources), maintenance (usability checks, feedback incorporation, relevance monitoring), retirement (communicate plan, archive data/code/docs, mark as retired; triggered by saturation, task irrelevance, or better alternatives).

**App. C List of Assessed Benchmarks** (header typo "Benchmakrs" in paper). 16 FM: AgentBench, ARC Challenge, BBQ, BIG-bench, BOLD, Codex HumanEval, DecodingTrust, GPQA, GSM8k, HellaSwag, Machiavelli, MLCommons AI Safety v0.5, MMLU, MMMU, TruthfulQA, WinoGrande. 8 non-FM: ALE, FinRL-Meta, MedMNIST v2, PDEBench, Procgen, RL Unplugged, SafeBench, Wordcraft.

- J.2 Implementation (10): evaluation code availability (unavailable code prevents scrutiny; ad-hoc reimplementation lets minor details shift measured performance [13]); replication script (inputs, outputs, eval code, hyperparameters, seeds for the paper's original results); accessibility of evaluation data/prompts/environment (private hosting acceptable when contamination is a concern); API-model support (15 pts = two or more APIs); local-model support; GUID/canary or encryption of instances (canary enables both filtering by model devs and post-hoc contamination detection; warns per [36] against non-key-protected obfuscation/compression because crawlers auto-decompress); training_on_test_set task ("post-hoc diagnosis of whether [...] benchmark data was used in model training" [75]); sensitive/harmful-content warnings (explicitly state presence or absence for tasks and outputs); release requirements (quotes MLCommons: publishers do not train on the benchmark and retract results on discovered contamination; gaming techniques may cause exclusion); build status (CI badge verifying latest commit runs).
- J.3 Documentation (19): requirements file; quick-start/demo; in-line comments; code documentation; test-task categories & rationale; normative assumptions (culture-dependent properties like politeness); limitations (15 pts = both applicability limits and design limits); construction-process documentation (decisions, rationale, trade-offs); persistent identifier (DOI for paper, dataset, and metadata; FAIR); standardized metadata (Croissant); data sources/collection (provenance, consent, licenses); preprocessing steps ("if none, state explicitly"); annotation process (guidelines, annotator qualifications/training, tools, quality control, inter-annotator agreement metrics); representativeness (sampling procedure, coverage, biases); standardized documentation (data cards); evaluation-metric documentation (exact formulas, parameters, thresholds); statistical significance for at least one model (15 pts = significance tests plus variance bounds or other uncertainty; 10 pts = bounding expected variation across training runs [40, 13]; "In cases where the benchmark is perfectly deterministic, this should be explicitly stated."); peer-reviewed acceptance (15 = journal/conference, 10 = workshop, 5 = under review); license specification.
- J.4 Maintenance (3): code usability checked within last year (main-file updates + passing build, or dated README statement; one-year threshold admitted as "somewhat arbitrary", footnotes 8-9); maintained feedback channel (GitHub issues acknowledged within three months; no open issues = full points, admitted imperfect proxy, footnotes 10-11); contact person (corresponding author, GitHub/website contact, or feedback form).

## Benchmark anatomy

(BetterBench is a meta-benchmark: it scores benchmarks, not models.)
- **n items:** 46 criteria x 24 benchmarks (16 FM + 8 non-FM) = ~1,104 criterion-level judgments (before n/a exclusions). Criteria split: 14 design, 10 implementation, 19 documentation, 3 maintenance (per App. J).
- **Construction method:** literature review across AI and non-AI benchmarking fields + unstructured interviews with 20+ representatives of five stakeholder groups; three iterative phases of criteria development with stakeholder validation (App. G); criteria restricted to developer-controlled, normative-consensus items (category a).
- **Who authored items:** the six authors (Stanford/Northeastern), informed by interviewees; no external item-writing.
- **Validation/review process:** three authors independently scored one common benchmark to calibrate; guidelines revised; each of the 24 benchmarks then scored independently by at least two authors with mandatory consensus; third-reviewer escalation defined but never used. No inter-rater agreement statistic (kappa/alpha) reported. Framework self-applied to BetterBench (App. I.2).
- **Human baseline:** none (n/a — humans are the raters; no human-performance target exists for a meta-benchmark).
- **Contamination defenses:** n/a for BetterBench itself ("Our benchmark does not evaluate AI models or include any examples which they could be contaminated by training on"); contamination is instead an assessed criterion (GUID/canary, encryption, training_on_test_set) fulfilled by only a few benchmarks (GPQA, BIG-bench cited as pioneers).
- **Scoring method:** discrete ordinal 0/5/10/15 with n/a; unweighted stage means; usability = applicable-criteria-count-weighted mean of implementation, documentation, maintenance (Eq. 1, App. F); mean >= 10 labeled "reasonably good".
- **Judge design:** human expert raters using rubric anchors per criterion (App. J); only developer-released sources admissible; no LLM judging anywhere.
- **Statistical reporting:** individual scores called deterministic; bootstrap resampling for the FM vs non-FM mean-difference (95% confidence region, not significant); Pearson correlations with p-values for design-usability association; no CIs on the headline stage means; no clustering considerations (criteria within a benchmark treated as independent contributions to averages).
- **Cost:** not reported; no significant compute ("standard laptops"); interviewees voluntary, uncompensated.

## Reported results

- Stage means (FM / non-FM / All, scale 0-15): Design 10.6 / 11.2 / 10.8; Implementation 5.5 / 7.5 / 6.2; Documentation 10.3 / 9.9 / 10.1; Maintenance 9.1 / 10.6 / 9.6 (Table 1). Text of Sec. 6 states overall design mean 10.7 (vs 10.8 in Table 1) and weighted usability mean 8.7.
- Criterion-level lows: replication script mean 3.75/15; statistical significance mean 5.62/15; standardized metadata fulfilled by almost no benchmark.
- 14/24 benchmarks performed no multiple evaluations of the same model and reported no statistical significance or uncertainty.
- 17/24 provide no easy-to-run replication script; a further 4/24 replicate only part of the results (so only 3/24 fully replicate).
- Build status: 3/24. GUID/encryption: "pioneered by only a few" (GPQA, BIG-bench).
- MMLU lowest weighted average 5.5; GPQA 11.0 — both reported side-by-side by GPT-4, Claude-3, Gemini, and included in UK AISI Inspect.
- FM vs non-FM difference in mean design and usability scores: not statistically significant (bootstrap resampling, 95% confidence level; Fig. 8).
- Design-usability Pearson correlation: FM rho = 0.730 (p = 0.001); non-FM rho = 0.477 (p = 0.279, not significant, n = 8); All rho = 0.693 (p < 0.001) (Table 2).
- Uncertainty caveat: only the FM/non-FM difference and the correlations carry inferential statistics; stage and criterion means are presented without error bars (defensible for a census of the 24 chosen benchmarks, but no generalization uncertainty is quantified).

## Limitations

**Admitted by the authors (Sec. 9, 10, footnotes):**
- Equal criterion weighting despite unequal effort and unequal contribution to quality.
- Four-level scale loses within-level nuance (barely-fulfilled = almost-fully-fulfilled = 10).
- The assessment is gameable by targeting easy criteria (they argue this still raises quality).
- Construct validity of assessed benchmarks is not evaluated — would require domain experts per field; framework is a minimum-quality blueprint, not a sufficiency test.
- Public benchmarks only; private benchmarks out of scope.
- Maintenance proxies are imperfect and thresholds arbitrary (1-year usability check, 3-month issue response; footnotes 8-11).
- Risk of misinterpretation: minimum quality assurance does not establish suitability for a concrete use case.
- Domain-expert criterion may disadvantage under-resourced researchers.

**Observed, not admitted:**
- No inter-rater reliability statistic despite a two-rater + consensus protocol; "consensus reached" hides initial disagreement rates, so scoring reproducibility by outsiders is unquantifiable.
- Ordinal 0/5/10/15 scores are averaged and fed into Pearson correlations as if interval-scaled; non-FM correlation rests on n = 8 (p = 0.279 is uninformative at that n, yet is tabled alongside significant results).
- Sample of 24 is hand-picked by popularity, not random, so criterion-prevalence claims ("14 out of 24") do not generalize to the benchmark population; no sampling frame is described.
- Internal inconsistencies: figure criteria sum to 47 vs claimed 46 (statistical significance appears under implementation in Fig. 3 but under documentation in App. J.3 #17); Sec. 6 text says design mean 10.7 while Table 1 says 10.8; flow diagram says 25 benchmarks scored vs 24 in the text; App. C header typo "Benchmakrs".
- Self-assessment (App. I.2) credits several YES answers to artifacts promised "upon publication" (replication script, build status, requirements file, quick-start) — the same leniency their rubric would penalize in others (rubric anchors require artifacts to exist).
- Per-criterion scores and justifications live only on the website, so the paper alone does not permit replication of Table 1/Fig. 7; code "will be available on GitHub upon publication".
- Peer-review acceptance as a quality criterion is partly circular (venue acceptance correlates with the other documented criteria) and time-dependent for new benchmarks.
- Scoring rewards mention-without-action (5 points for acknowledging a problem), which can inflate scores for well-written but unimproved benchmarks.
- Assessment of "validated automatic evaluation" and "input sensitivity" is binary-rubric shallow; it checks that validation happened, not that it was any good.

## Implications for CRUCIBLE-CHAIN

1. **State determinism explicitly and split the noise budget (App. J.3 #17).** BetterBench's statistical-significance criterion gives full marks only for significance tests plus variance bounds, and explicitly says a perfectly deterministic benchmark should say so. CRUCIBLE-CHAIN's labels are deterministic but model outputs are not: document "label noise ~0 by construction; all residual variance is model-side sampling variance", and report intra-model variance from >= 5 repeated runs per model x condition (varying seed/temperature) next to inter-model gaps, exactly the intra-vs-inter framing of Sec. 7. This turns the generator's determinism from an implicit property into a scored, auditable claim.
2. **Size the benchmark from the 1%-detectability rule, clustered by template (Sec. 5).** BetterBench: "Benchmarks should be designed so that a 1% improvement can be reliably detected", dependent on data size and task diversity. For the question of record: with target single-digit pass rates (p ~ 0.05), the binomial SE is sqrt(p(1-p)/n_eff); detecting a 1-point difference needs SE <= ~0.5pt, i.e., n_eff ~ 1,900 independent items, and detecting a 3-5 point model gap needs n_eff ~ 200-500. But instances within a template are correlated, so n_eff is bounded by independent templates: 8 templates cannot support significance claims regardless of instance count. Plan on the order of 30-50 independent templates x 20+ instances x >= 3 repeats, and compute CIs with template-level cluster bootstrap — publishing this power analysis is itself a BetterBench design criterion (informed metric choice + significance).
3. **Adopt the contamination stack BetterBench found almost nobody uses (J.2 #6-7, Sec. 7).** Only a few of 24 benchmarks (GPQA, BIG-bench) used GUIDs/canaries or encryption, and BetterBench scores three distinct defenses: canary GUID in every file, key-protected encryption of test instances (explicitly not plain obfuscation, since crawlers auto-decompress [36]), and a training_on_test_set probe task. CRUCIBLE-CHAIN's generator makes all three nearly free: stamp a canary into every generated artifact, publish only encrypted instance sets with the key gated, hold back a private seed pool for fresh regeneration each release, and emit a small training_on_test_set split. This also remediates the observed saturation cause — leaked method recipes and answer menus are a contamination-by-publication failure; the release requirement mechanism (J.2 #9, App. H) gives a place to forbid training on or republishing instance text.
4. **Report random and human performance levels per condition (J.1 #10-11).** BetterBench requires an explicit random baseline and a human level with methodology (expert vs average). For CRUCIBLE-CHAIN, the random chain-pass rate is computable exactly from the generator (product over 5-8 stages of per-stage guess rates — likely << 1%; publish the number per template, since non-compensatory scoring makes it a powerful floor). For humans, either run domain-expert scientists on a stratified subsample (report per-condition pass rates, especially the flawed-premise/refusal condition where "correct" behavior is refusal) or write the explicit n/a justification BetterBench demands. Without these, single-digit frontier scores are uninterpretable — nobody can tell "hard" from "broken".
5. **Treat input sensitivity as both a robustness measure and the anti-leak fix (J.1 #12).** BetterBench requires multiple semantically equivalent input variations with documented count and design, to measure capability rather than "an artifact" of phrasing. Because CRUCIBLE-CHAIN saturated when prompts leaked method recipes and answer menus, generate k >= 3 surface paraphrases per instance (no recipe scaffolding, free-response instead of answer menus), report score variance across phrasings, and use that variance as the intra-model noise estimate feeding implication 2. Removing answer menus is also what converts each stage back into an open judgment call with an attractive wrong path.
6. **Validate the stage-level scorer like an automatic-evaluation claim (J.1 #13).** The deterministic final-answer check is self-validating, but stage-level credit assignment and refusal detection in the flawed-premise condition are parsing/judging steps BetterBench would require to be validated: human-audit a sample of transcripts per condition, report agreement between the automated stage scorer and human adjudication, and document failure modes. Score the audit before scaling the campaign — a mis-parsed refusal flips the sign of the flawed-premise condition.
7. **Bank the cheap usability points that 21/24 benchmarks miss (Sec. 6-7, App. I).** Replication script (mean 3.75/15 across benchmarks), build status (3/24), requirements file, quick-start, feedback channel, named contact, license, DOI, Croissant metadata: each is a rubric line in BetterBench and each is hours of work. Ship the full App. I.1 checklist, filled out, inside the CRUCIBLE-CHAIN release (the 828-run campaign's release/1.0.0 package is the natural place), plus a one-command script that regenerates the paper's numbers; this alone would place CRUCIBLE-CHAIN above every assessed benchmark's implementation score.
8. **Version the saturation event instead of patching it (Sec. 5 versioning; App. B retirement).** BetterBench's lifecycle says: saturated benchmarks get retired and marked as retired; major changes require a new version (AgentBench v0.1 -> v0.2 precedent). The 94-100% frontier saturation of the current 8x~18 release means: freeze and label it v1 (retired/saturated, with the saturation post-mortem documented as a limitation), and release the hardened design as v2 with changed instance distributions — never silently overwrite, or comparability of all prior runs is destroyed and the documented-limitations criterion is failed.
9. **Pre-commit interpretation guidance (J.1 #7).** BetterBench requires stating how scores should AND should not be used. For a constructed-truth benchmark this matters doubly: state that chain-pass rate measures multi-stage judgment under adversarial framing on synthetic-but-internally-valid science, and explicitly disclaim inference to real-lab discovery competence (the construct-validity gap Sec. 8 flags as unsolved). This inoculates CRUCIBLE-CHAIN against the misuse pattern Sec. 7 documents for MMLU/GPQA reporting.
